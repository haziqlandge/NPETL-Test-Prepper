# Week 4 — E-Marketing and Supply Chain Management (Lectures 1 to 5)

## In one breath

Week 4 has two halves. The first half is e-marketing: how a company uses the internet to find, win and keep customers, using the AIDA steps (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action). The second half is supply chain management (SCM): the whole line of firms that moves a product from the first supplier to the final customer, the information passed along that line, and the warehouse, transport and delivery systems that move the goods.

**The big analogy:** one Swiggy or Flipkart order, from the first ad you tap to the box at your door. The ad, the app notification and the "you bought this before" offer are e-marketing. The vegetable supplier, the warehouse, the delivery van and the bank transfer behind the scenes are the supply chain. Every question this week is about one stop on that journey.

## Concept cards

### 1. E-Marketing

**Purpose of marketing**
- What it is: Marketing has two jobs. Sell existing products in existing markets, and develop new products or reach new markets.
- Think of it as: A kirana shop that sells its usual rice to regulars and starts stocking millets for a new crowd.
- Spot it in a question: "existing products, existing markets" is job one. "new products, new markets" is job two.

**AIDA model** (AIDA = Attention, Interest, Desire, Action)
- What it is: The four steps a seller takes a stranger through before they buy.
- Think of it as: A movie trailer. It grabs your eye, shows why the film is worth it, makes you want it, then tells you where to book.
- Spot it in a question: "gets noticed" = Attention. "shows advantages and benefits" = Interest. "convinces the customer the product suits their need" = Desire. "leads to a purchase" = Action.

**eMarketing (electronic marketing)**
- What it is: Running AIDA through the internet as one more channel, next to shops, TV and phone.
- Think of it as: A WhatsApp broadcast list added to a shop's signboard and newspaper ad. It adds a channel; it does not replace the others.
- Spot it in a question: "additional communication channel" or "internet used to realise AIDA".

**Marketing paradigm shift (mass to mass customised)**
- What it is: Marketing moved from one message for everyone to a product built for each person, at mass-production cost.
- Think of it as: A tiffin service that cooked one menu for the whole colony, then started asking each family what they want.
- Spot it in a question: the end point is always mass customised marketing. The six eras, in order:
  - Undifferentiated mass marketing (1940-50): one product, one message for all. Think: the only ice-cream flavour in a village shop.
  - Differentiated mass marketing (1950-60): several versions for groups, still mass. Think: a cloth shop's cotton, silk and polyester lines.
  - Segment-oriented marketing (1960-70): split the market into segments. Think: a canteen with student, teacher and staff menus.
  - Niche marketing (1970-80): serve one small specialised group that big firms ignore. Think: a bakery making only sugar-free cakes.
  - Marketing for the individual customer (1980-90): a market of one. Think: a tailor who cuts only your suit.
  - Mass customised marketing (1990 onwards): personal feel at mass cost. Think: a T-shirt printed with your name, at a normal price, delivered in three days.

**Internet as an enabler of mass customised marketing**
- What it is: The internet brings three gains: reduced cost, reduced time and higher outreach.
- Think of it as: One WhatsApp message typed once, reaching 500 people in seconds with no printing or postage.
- Spot it in a question: the right option lists lower cost, less time and bigger reach. Higher cost, slower speed or a smaller audience is the reverse.

**Characteristics of eMarketing** (six)
- Associative information acquisition: the customer hops from link to link following their own interests. Spot it: "jumps from site to site", "follows associations".
- Market pull and customised push: the customer asks (pull), or the company sends tailored messages (push). Spot it: "customer asks" vs "tailored message sent".
- Integration of customers into value chain creation: customers help design the product. Think: choosing your own pizza toppings so the kitchen cooks what you picked. Spot it: "customer suggestions go into the product".
- One-to-one marketing: each customer is treated as an individual. Think: the tea-stall owner who knows you take less sugar. Spot it: "individual", "tailored to the person".
- Ubiquity: reachable from anywhere, at any time. Think: a shop open in your pocket, 24 hours a day. Spot it: "anywhere", "any time".
- Disintermediation: buyer and seller deal directly, with no middlemen. Think: buying mangoes from the farmer's cart. Spot it: "without intermediaries". The opposite is intermediation.

**Online customer model** (five-stage ladder: surfer, consumer, prosumer, buyer, key online customer)
- What it is: The path a visitor climbs. Each stage needs a different facility from the company.
- Think of it as: A cinema fan. First a passer-by, then a bookmarker, then a fan who suggests scenes, then a ticket buyer, then the loyal guest who comes every Friday.
- Spot it in a question: match the behaviour described to one stage. Memory: Surf, Save, Shape, Shop, Stay.

**Online surfer**
- What it is: A visitor who comes just to get acquainted with a brand. Surfs aimlessly and jumps from site to site.
- Think of it as: Window shopping in a mall with no list.
- Spot it in a question: "get acquainted", "surf aimlessly". To create surfers, the company chooses a domain name (an independent marketing domain or a sub-domain inside the main company site), protects it and publicises it.

**Online consumer**
- What it is: A surfer who has been drawn into goal-directed, repeated contact. Keeps the address in bookmarks, reads more, downloads and prints.
- Think of it as: A bookshop regular who keeps coming back for the same author.
- Spot it in a question: "bookmarks", "repeated, goal-directed contact", "reading, downloading, printing". Facilities that build consumers: catalogues, presentations, videos and simulations, brochure downloads, interactive applications, and a feedback facility.

**Online prosumer** (producer plus consumer)
- What it is: A customer who wants standard products tailored to personal taste, and is willing to share personal information to get them.
- Think of it as: Configuring your own laptop on the company site, where your feedback changes the next model.
- Spot it in a question: "tailored", "shares personal information", "suggestions go into the value chain". Facilities: individualise a product and visualise its effect, online community, two-way feedback, suggestions fed into the value chain.

**Online buyer**
- What it is: A customer who makes a purchase online.
- Think of it as: The person at the billing counter with the card already out.
- Spot it in a question: "makes a purchase online". Facilities: shopping cart, payment, delivery tracking, and keeping the relationship going.

**Key online customer**
- What it is: A returning customer who makes multiple purchases.
- Think of it as: The tailor's regular who gets a birthday discount every year.
- Spot it in a question: "returning", "multiple purchases", "CRM", "special offers", "satisfaction survey". Facilities: CRM, personal communication, special offers, satisfaction survey.

**Activities under marketing** (seven)
- What it is: The seven jobs under the marketing umbrella: interactive marketing, sales force automation, sales management, customer relationship management, market research and forecast, advertising and promotion, product management.
- Think of it as: The departments of a mall: help desk, sales floor, billing counter, loyalty desk, survey team, hoardings and the product team.
- Spot it in a question: match the verb in the question to the activity below.
  - Interactive marketing: two-way talk between seller and customer. Think: a shopkeeper chatting on WhatsApp. Spot it: "chat", "discussion groups", "web forms and questionnaires", "instant messaging", "e-mail correspondence".
  - Sales force automation: capturing and analysing sales data in real time over the internet. Spot it: "real-time sales data".
  - Sales management: online decision support for managers. Spot it: "decision support".
  - Customer relationship management (CRM): looking after existing customers, their history, preferences and loyalty. Think: a neighbourhood store's ledger of what each family buys. Spot it: "relationship", "customer data".
  - Market research and forecast: collecting customer and market data to predict demand. Spot it: "data collection", "forecast", "large sample".
  - Advertising and promotion: push, pull and the promotion types (cards below).
  - Product management: product design and business model online (cards below).

**Explicit data**
- What it is: Information the customer gives on purpose.
- Think of it as: The feedback card you fill in at a restaurant.
- Spot it in a question: "questionnaire answer", "stated preference", "rating given".

**Implicit data**
- What it is: Information collected by watching what the customer does (clicks, time on a page), without them saying it.
- Think of it as: A shopkeeper who notices you always stop at the sweets shelf.
- Spot it in a question: "clicks recorded", "the customer never provided it". Clicks are the classic example.

**Structured data**
- What it is: Data with a fixed shape, in rows and columns.
- Think of it as: A class attendance register with a name, a roll number and a yes or no per day.
- Spot it in a question: "database", "fields", "rows and columns".

**Unstructured data**
- What it is: Free-form data with no fixed shape.
- Think of it as: A personal diary written in any style.
- Spot it in a question: "free text", "reviews", "e-mails", "social posts", "photos".

**Push principle**
- What it is: The provider starts the promotion and the customer receives information they did not ask for. This is where spam comes from. A good push channel lets the customer subscribe and unsubscribe.
- Think of it as: A WhatsApp broadcast you never joined, with or without a "reply STOP" line.
- Spot it in a question: "provider initiates", "unsolicited", "no way to stop". If the question asks what is missing, the answer is the subscribe and unsubscribe facility.

**Customised push**
- What it is: A push message tailored to the person. It is still push, because the provider starts it.
- Think of it as: A shop texting you about rain jackets because you looked at one last week.
- Spot it in a question: "push" plus "tailored to the user".

**Spam**
- What it is: Unwanted, mass, unsolicited messages with no easy way out. The bad form of push.
- Think of it as: Pamphlets pushed under every door, every day.
- Spot it in a question: "inundated", "unwanted", "no way to stop".

**Pull principle**
- What it is: The user starts the demand for information. The user selects or follows their own associations, and may pay fees.
- Think of it as: Choosing your own book at a library and paying the membership fee.
- Spot it in a question: "user requests", "user chooses", "user pays a fee", "user subscribes to requested reports".

**Promotion types** (six)
- Banner advertising and buttons: ads placed on someone else's page. Think: a poster on a bus-stop wall. Spot it: "banner", "button".
- Nanosite: a new site shown without leaving the current page. Think: a mini stall pitched inside the same shop. Spot it: "without leaving the current page".
- Microsite: a specially designed separate site for one campaign or product. Think: a pop-up shop in another lane of the mall. Spot it: "special designed website".
- Branded content: a part of the company's own website dedicated to the brand. Think: a brand corner in a department store. Spot it: "part of the website dedicated to the brand".
- Embedded content: promotion placed inside the content: keyword search results and promoted news. Think: a sponsored item styled like news. Spot it: "key word search", "promoted news".

**Targeting components for promotion** (five)
- Community targeting: customised ads for special virtual community websites. Think: a cricket club's notice board with ads for bats. Spot it: "virtual community".
- Content targeting: ads on other organisations' websites related to the product. Think: a shoe-polish ad in a fashion magazine. Spot it: "related websites of other organisations".
- Context targeting: ads shown only to people already looking for information on the subject. Think: an umbrella seller at the bus stop on a rainy day. Spot it: "already searching right now".
- Demographic and psychographic targeting: targeting by background. Demographic = who you are (age, income). Psychographic = what you value or enjoy. Think: a toy ad in a school newsletter. Spot it: "particular background", "lifestyle".
- Online behaviour targeting: targeting by browsing captured through cookies (small files a website stores in your browser). Think: a shop assistant who noticed your ten minutes at the shoe rack. Spot it: "browsing behaviour", "cookies", "browsing logs".

**Product management** (four items)
- What it is: Making the product work online: business model, design, store locations and payment.
- Think of it as: Arranging a shop's shelves, billing counter and address board.
- Spot it in a question: any of these four.
  - B-to-C (business-to-consumer) model: the business sells straight to end customers. Think: a wholesaler's shop selling to households. Spot it: "individual consumers". The opposite is B-to-B (business-to-business).
  - Interactive design: product pages where the customer configures and tries the product. Think: a car website where you change colour and wheels. Spot it: "configure the product on the page".
  - Store locator: finds the nearest physical outlet that stocks the product. Think: the "find a branch" button on a bank site. Spot it: "nearest outlet".
  - Online payment: paying for the order on the site. Spot it: "pay online".

**Four Ps of marketing** (Product, Price, Place, Promotion)
- What it is: The classic marketing mix.
- Think of it as: A shopkeeper's four decisions: what to sell, what to charge, where to sell it, how to tell people.
- Spot it in a question: "classic 4Ps". Packaging, personalisation and process are not on the list.

### 2. Supply chain and information flow

**Supply chain (SC)**
- What it is: The network of organisations, people, activities, information and resources that moves a product or service from supplier to customer.
- Think of it as: The relay that brings a cotton shirt from the farm, through the mill and cutting unit, to your wardrobe.
- Spot it in a question: "many organisations", "supplier to customer". Supply chain = suppliers + logistics + customers.

**Supply chain management (SCM)**
- What it is: Running that network well: planning, buying, making, moving and tracking across companies.
- Think of it as: A wedding planner coordinating caterers, decorators and drivers so everything arrives on time.
- Spot it in a question: "managing", "coordinating across partners". The supply chain is the thing; SCM is the managing of it.

**Value chain** (the picture on slide 28; Michael Porter's classic model, not named on the slide)
- What it is: The steps inside one firm that add value. Primary activities go from making to serving the customer. Support activities run in the background.
- Think of it as: A dosa stall. Buy batter, make the dosa, pack it, sell it, follow up. Accounts and hiring work sits behind the counter.
- Spot it in a question: "inside one firm" points to the value chain. "many firms from supplier to customer" points to the supply chain.
- Primary activities: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service.
- Support activities: firm infrastructure, human resource development, technology development, procurement.

**Order flow and delivery flow**
- What it is: Orders move upstream, back toward suppliers. Deliveries move downstream, forward to customers.
- Think of it as: Requests climbing the stairs, parcels coming down them.
- Spot it in a question: "upstream" means the supplier side. "downstream" means the customer side.

**Four main cycles of the supply chain**
- What it is: Four loops, each sitting between two neighbouring links of the chain.
- Think of it as: Four legs of a relay race, each one handing the baton to the next.
- Spot it in a question: name the two links the question mentions, then pick the cycle that sits between them.
  - Customer buying cycle: between the customer and the dealer. Steps: marketing, sales, after-sales support. Spot it: "after-sales". Procurement is never in this cycle.
  - Replenishment cycle: between the dealer and the distributor. It restocks the dealer and chooses the distribution logistics: online, offline or hybrid. Spot it: "restock", "distribution logistics".
  - Manufacturing cycle: between the distributor and the manufacturer. Production options: on-demand production and just-in-time. Spot it: "production option".
  - Procurement cycle: between the manufacturer and the supplier. Buying raw materials, using the sell side, buy side or marketplace strategies. Spot it: "raw materials", "supplier".

**Sell side** (procurement strategy)
- What it is: The supplier puts its catalogue online to sell to many buyers.
- Think of it as: A wholesaler posting its price list online for any shop to order from.
- Spot it in a question: "supplier builds an online catalogue to sell to many buyers".

**Buy side** (procurement strategy)
- What it is: The buyer runs online buying to find and choose the best seller.
- Think of it as: A purchase department that sends an online request for quotes to several vendors and picks one.
- Spot it in a question: "buyer uses online tools to find and choose suppliers".

**Marketplace** (procurement strategy)
- What it is: One online place where many buyers and suppliers meet, and suppliers compete.
- Think of it as: A wholesale mandi where many traders bid for the same goods.
- Spot it in a question: "digital marketplace where multiple suppliers compete".

The lecture names the three procurement strategies but does not define them. The meanings above are the standard e-procurement ones.

**Just-in-time (JIT)**
- What it is: Parts arrive exactly when they are needed, so stock is kept very low.
- Think of it as: A tiffin delivered just before lunch rather than a week's supply in the fridge.
- Spot it in a question: "exactly when needed", "minimal stock".

**SCOR model** (Supply Chain Operations Reference)
- What it is: A standard reference model that sorts supply chain work into Plan, Source, Make and Deliver, with numbered process steps (1 to 14 on the slide).
- Think of it as: A restaurant. Plan = menu and forecast. Source = buying vegetables. Make = cooking. Deliver = serving the table.
- Spot it in a question: "buying from suppliers" = Source. "production" = Make. "order supply to the customer" = Deliver. "forecasts and opportunities" = Plan.

**Purchaser-supplier documents**
- What it is: The paper trail between a buyer (purchaser) and a supplier.
- Think of it as: The order slip, the reply, the bill and the receipts between a shop and a wholesaler.
- Spot it in a question: match the job the document does.
  - Request for quote (RFQ): the buyer asks for price and terms before ordering. The supplier's reply is the "response to RFQ".
  - Purchase order (PO): the buyer's formal order. A PO change modifies an order already placed.
  - PO acknowledgement and status response: the supplier confirms the order and reports where it stands.
  - Shipping notice: the supplier says the goods have left and what is inside. Spot it: "goods left".
  - Invoice: the supplier's bill. Spot it: "amount due".
  - Receiving notice: the buyer says the goods arrived. Spot it: "goods arrived".
  - Payment advice: the buyer says payment is on its way. Spot it: "payment coming".

**B2B payment flow** (business-to-business, with banks)
- What it is: How money moves between purchaser, supplier and their banks.
- Think of it as: Paying a vendor through your bank, with a bank-to-bank transfer behind the scenes.
- Spot it in a question:
  - Payment authorisation: the purchaser tells its own bank to pay.
  - Electronic transfer of funds: money moves electronically from bank to bank.
  - Payment remittance notice: the slip that says which invoices the payment covers.
  - Deposit notice: the supplier's bank confirms the money has been credited.

**Information sharing**
- What it is: Passing the right data between partners in time: sales, stock, forecasts and promotions.
- Think of it as: A whole village checking the same weather forecast, so nobody over-plants or under-plants.
- Spot it in a question: shared data gives less variability, better forecasts, coordinated plans, easier item location, faster reaction and shorter lead times (six benefits, in the facts table).

**Variability**
- What it is: Orders and stock swing much more than real customer buying does.
- Think of it as: The back of a bus lurching far more than the front when the driver brakes.
- Spot it in a question: "swings", "fluctuations", "orders far bigger than sales".
- Contributing factors (six). Each one is a cause, not a fix.
  - Demand forecasting: each partner guesses from the orders it receives, so guesses pile up. Think: the telephone game.
  - Lead time: long waits make each partner order extra. Think: buying extra sugar because delivery takes two weeks.
  - Batch ordering: ordering in lumps. Think: a family buying a year's salt in one trip.
  - Price fluctuation: discounts make buyers stock up, then stop. Think: "buy 2, get 1 free" makes everyone hoard.
  - Inflated orders: ordering more than needed to be safe. Think: everyone buying two bags of sugar when they hear it may run out.
  - Lack of centralised information: nobody sees real demand in one place, so everyone guesses. Think: each shop keeping its own sales book.

**Lead time** (order, delivery and production lead time)
- What it is: The time between placing an order and receiving the goods. The lecture splits it into order lead time (the order travelling up), delivery lead time (the goods travelling down) and production lead time (the factory making the goods).
- Think of it as: The wait from pressing "order" on Swiggy until the food arrives. Cooking time plus rider time.
- Spot it in a question: "time from order to delivery". Shorter lead time is a benefit of information sharing.

**Bullwhip effect**
- What it is: Variability grows as you move up the supply chain, from customer to retailer, wholesaler, distributor and factory. The factory sees the biggest swings.
- Think of it as: A small flick of the whip handle makes the tip crack hard.
- Spot it in a question: "variability increases as we travel up the supply chain", "factory orders swing far more than customer sales".
- Consequences (four): increased safety stock, reduced service level, inefficient allocation of resources, increased transportation costs.

**e-Supply chain (e-SCM, electronic SCM)**
- What it is: The supply chain run on electronic, real-time updates from consumers all the way to suppliers.
- Think of it as: A live scoreboard shared across the stadium, instead of waiting for a call from the boundary.
- Spot it in a question: "real-time updates across the chain", "greater ability to fill orders", "better understanding of customer needs".

**Two integrated solutions** (inter-enterprise collaborative system vs centralised SCM system)
- What it is: Two ways to connect partners. Either the companies link their own systems as equals, or one central SCM system serves everyone.
- Think of it as: Neighbours sharing a group chat (equal partners) vs one housing society office holding everyone's records (central).
- Spot it in a question: "partners collaborate across companies" points to inter-enterprise. "one centralised system" points to the central SCM system.

**Strategic level** (SCM objective)
- What it is: Long-term choices: objectives, policies (service levels), operating footprint and network design.
- Think of it as: Deciding where to build warehouses for the next five years.
- Spot it in a question: "network design", "service level policy", "operating footprint".

**Tactical level** (SCM objective)
- What it is: Medium-term plans that match supply to demand. Outcomes: demand forecast, production, procurement and logistics plan, inventory targets.
- Think of it as: Deciding how much stock to hold for the festival season.
- Spot it in a question: "demand forecast", "inventory targets".

**Operational execution level** (SCM objective)
- What it is: Day-to-day running. Outcomes: work-centre scheduling, order and inventory tracking, the order cycle, material movement.
- Think of it as: Today's kitchen order sheet: who cooks what now, and where each order is.
- Spot it in a question: "scheduling", "tracking", "order cycle", "material movement".
- The slide uses the same objective wording ("deploy resources to match supply to demand") for tactical and operational. The outcomes are what differ.

**SCM functional processes** (six)
- Strategic sourcing and procurement; forecast and demand planning; customer order fulfilment and service; distribution network and warehouse operations; transportation and shipment management; production logistics.
- Spot it in a question: match the job in the question to one of these six phrases.

**Example of an SCM system**
- What it is: The warehouse system (WMS), the transport system (TMS) and the back-end corporate systems (ERP) exchange customer orders, shipping notifications and optimised shipping plans.
- Think of it as: The kitchen, the delivery fleet and the accounts office passing slips to each other.
- Spot it in a question: the three systems named together.

**e-SCM challenges** (five)
- Planning, selecting and implementing SCM solutions.
- Creating a real-time SCM infrastructure.
- Coping with frequent technology changes.
- Coping with the technology of multiple supply chain partners.
- Introducing a new supply chain partner.
- Spot it in a question: "frequent change", "many partners' technology", "new partner joins".

### 3. Integration and interoperability

**Supply chain integration**
- What it is: How far a firm truly collaborates with its partners, managing processes across companies, so that products, services, information, money and decisions flow at low cost and high speed.
- Think of it as: A cricket team following one plan, not three separate games.
- Spot it in a question: "collaborate with partners", "low cost, high speed", "maximum value to the customer".
- It has two parts: information integration (technical) and collaboration (managerial).

**Information integration** (the technical half of integration)
- What it is: Systems linked so that data flows between them.
- Think of it as: Two shops' billing machines syncing stock counts automatically.
- Spot it in a question: "technical issues", "systems exchange data".

**Collaboration** (the managerial half of integration)
- What it is: The people and contract side: collaborative communication, a coordinating contract, information sharing, joint decision making, joint knowledge creation and resource sharing.
- Think of it as: Two families agreeing one wedding budget and writing down who does what.
- Spot it in a question: "contract", "joint decisions", "shared resources", "communication".

**Supply chain interoperability**
- What it is: Two or more systems can exchange information and also use what they received.
- Think of it as: Two people who hear each other and understand the words.
- Spot it in a question: "exchange and use the information". It has two layers: technical and semantic.

**Technical interoperability**
- What it is: Hardware and software are compatible, so the systems can connect at all.
- Think of it as: A charger plug that fits the wall socket.
- Spot it in a question: "hardware and software compatibility", "cannot connect".

**Semantic interoperability**
- What it is: Both systems mean the same thing by the same concept.
- Think of it as: One shop writes "1 pack" meaning 10 pieces, another means 12. The paper arrives, but it is misread.
- Spot it in a question: data transfers fine but the two systems call the same item by different names or meanings (a "pallet" in one system, a "carton" in the other). That is a semantic problem.

**B2B integration challenges** (five)
- Diverse information formats; a large and dynamic information space; lack of standards for semantic integration; fast and secure transmission needs; reliable transmission needs.
- Spot it in a question: a list of why business-to-business integration is hard.

**Interoperability of data** (the first dimension of interoperability)
- What it is: Finding and sharing information from heterogeneous databases (different machines, operating systems and database software), and resolving differences in meaning and structure. EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) and XML (eXtensible Markup Language) are the standards that solve this level.
- Think of it as: Two ledgers written in different scripts, plus a translator who makes "amount" mean the same thing in both.
- Spot it in a question: "different database platforms", "exchanging messages or inventory data", "EDI", "XML".

**Interoperability of service** (the second dimension of interoperability)
- What it is: Identifying, combining and running applications that were built separately. It needs a common architecture: CORBA (Common Object Broker Architecture, as the slide writes it), SOA (Service Oriented Architecture) and cloud.
- Think of it as: One remote control that works a TV, a speaker and a fan, made by three different companies.
- Spot it in a question: "designed and implemented independently", "operate together", "architecture".

**CORBA**
- What it is: A common architecture that lets software components on different machines call each other.
- Think of it as: A shared interpreter at a multilingual conference desk.
- Spot it in a question: named as an architecture for service interoperability.

**SOA (Service Oriented Architecture)**
- What it is: Software built as reusable services that talk to each other over agreed rules.
- Think of it as: A thali where each katori comes from a different kitchen, but the whole plate works.
- Spot it in a question: "services", "composing applications".

**Integration vs interoperability**
- What it is: Integration is the deeper, planned tie-up. Interoperability is the ability to exchange and use data. Two integrated systems are always interoperable. Two interoperable systems are not necessarily integrated.
- Think of it as: Two friends who can text each other (interoperable), vs two friends who share a bank account and a plan (integrated). Anyone who shares the account can text; not everyone who texts shares the account.
- Spot it in a question: "always", "necessarily", "identical", "eliminates". Check the direction: integrated leads to interoperable, never the reverse.

**Planning collaboration**
- What it is: Partners agree on production planning: what to produce, when, where and how.
- Think of it as: A caterer and a banquet hall agreeing the menu, date and room set-up together.
- Spot it in a question: "what, when, where and how to produce", "production planning decision".

**Inventory collaboration**
- What it is: Partners agree on ordering: when to order and how much.
- Think of it as: A kitchen and a grocer agreeing a fixed weekly top-up.
- Spot it in a question: "when to order", "how much to order".

**Collaboration strategies, Types 0 to 3** (the 2x2 grid from Holweg, Disney, Holmström and Småros, 2005)
- What it is: The four strategies, placed by whether planning is shared (up or down) and whether inventory is shared (left or right).
- Think of it as: A four-square board. Planning yes or no on one axis, inventory yes or no on the other.
- Spot it in a question: find whether planning, inventory, both or neither is shared, then map it to Type 1, 2, 3 or 0.

**Type 0: Traditional supply chain**
- What it is: No planning collaboration and no inventory collaboration. Each level orders and restocks on its own, ignoring the levels up and down the chain. No visibility, no collaboration, and the bullwhip effect. The lecture says most supply chains still work this way.
- Think of it as: Each shop in a row placing its own order by guessing, never asking the shop next to it.
- Spot it in a question: "without considering upstream or downstream situations", "no visibility".

**Type 1: Collaborative planning and forecasting** (often written CPFR, the R for replenishment is not on the slide)
- What it is: Planning collaboration yes, inventory collaboration no. Retailer and supplier still order independently, but exchange demand information and action plans to align forecasts for capacity and long-term planning.
- Think of it as: A shop and its supplier sharing the festival forecast each month, but each still placing its own orders.
- Spot it in a question: "order independently but exchange demand information and action plans", "align forecasts".
- Needs: the right information at the right time from all partners, shared information standards, and undistorted information.

**Type 2: Vendor managed inventory (VMI)**
- What it is: Inventory collaboration yes, planning collaboration no. The supplier generates the replenishment order and takes responsibility for the retailer's stock and service level.
- Think of it as: A milkman who decides how many packets each house needs and keeps the fridge stocked.
- Spot it in a question: "replenishment order given to the supplier", "supplier responsible for the retailer's inventory", "consignment stock", "supplier may not share production planning".

**Type 3: Synchronised supply chain**
- What it is: Both planning and inventory collaboration. The supplier merges the replenishment decision with its own material planning, and uses its view of the customer's stock to plan its own supply operations.
- Think of it as: A dairy planning its feed and milking around exactly how much milk each shop needs next week.
- Spot it in a question: "merges replenishment with the supplier's material planning", "uses this visibility in planning its own supply operation".

**Key factors that guide the collaboration strategy** (three)
- Geographical dispersion of customers and suppliers' plants: close, dedicated supply makes synchronised production easier.
- Demand pattern: stable demand gains the most from removing the bullwhip effect.
- Product characteristics: a longer shelf life or selling period, and higher value, make collaboration and tight inventory control worthwhile.
- Think of it as: Milk from a dairy next door with steady demand is easy to plan together. A one-season fashion item made far away is not.
- Spot it in a question: clues about location, demand stability, shelf life or value.

**Consignment stock**
- What it is: Stock held at the retailer that still belongs to the supplier until the retailer uses it. The retailer pays when it uses the stock. Once the stock enters the retailer's inventory, the supplier reduces its own stock level.
- Think of it as: Shoes on a shop shelf that belong to the brand. The shop pays only for the pairs that sell.
- Spot it in a question: "pays when used", "supplier-owned stock sitting at the retailer".

**VMI: how the arrangement runs**
- What it is: Two linked workflows: a replenishment cycle (restock) and a payment cycle (invoice and pay). The supplier checks the retailer's stock every 24 hours, calculates the reorder point, generates the purchase order, dispatches consignment stock and updates the consignment status. The retailer keeps selling while stock is available. When stock runs out, it uses consignment stock, updates its stock level, informs the supplier, gets the invoice and pays on use.
- Think of it as: A shared fridge. The milkman restocks, and the house pays for what it drinks.
- Spot it in a question: "replenishment cycle", "payment cycle", "reorder point", "every 24 hours".
- The lecture's UML (Unified Modeling Language) sequence diagram lists the messages in this order: GetStockLevel, SetStockLevel, GeneratePurchaseOrder, SetConsignmentDetail, AcknowledgeConsignmentReceipt, SetConsignmentUsageBeginInfo, SendInvoice.

**VMI integration issue 1: information integration**
- What it is: The supplier's systems link with the retailer's to share point-of-sale (POS) data and inventory status, because the supplier is responsible for the retailer's service level.
- Think of it as: The milkman getting a live view of the house's fridge.
- Spot it in a question: "share POS data and inventory status".

**VMI integration issue 2: planning synchronisation**
- What it is: The supplier forecasts demand and sends a purchase order. The retailer may ask for a change (for example, the fill rate is not met, or it expects demand to rise), or accept the order.
- Think of it as: A caterer sends a quote and the client asks to add 20 guests.
- Spot it in a question: "fill rate", "retailer requests to modify the purchase order".

**VMI integration issue 3: workflow coordination**
- What it is: Linking the replenishment and payment cycles so that each activity follows the last.
- Think of it as: A relay where the next runner starts only after the baton is handed over.
- Spot it in a question: "workflow", "activities linked together", "two cycles".

**VMI integration issue 4: monitoring and measurement**
- What it is: Watching three flows (information, material and credit) so they stay in step. If the retailer uses consignment stock but does not update the VMI system, the replenishment cycle is disrupted.
- Think of it as: A courier tracking three things: the order details, the parcel and the payment.
- Spot it in a question: "forgets to report", "not updated in the VMI system", "three flows synchronised".

**Three flows in VMI**
- What it is: Information flow (data), material flow (goods) and credit flow (money). All three must line up for VMI to work.
- Think of it as: An online order. The order details, the parcel and the card payment all have to arrive together.
- Spot it in a question: "information, material and credit flows".

### 4. Logistics and distribution

**Logistics**
- What it is: Moving goods efficiently and cheaply from the source of supply, through the factory, to the point of consumption, while still giving the customer an acceptable service. Logistics = materials management + distribution. The definition comes from the Rushton, Croucher and Baker handbook.
- Think of it as: The delivery rider plus the godown, working as one.
- Spot it in a question: "efficient transfer of goods", "cost-effective", "acceptable service".

**Materials management** (the inbound half of logistics)
- What it is: Bringing materials in: raw materials, components, packaging and bought-in parts.
- Think of it as: The canteen buying vegetables and spices before it cooks.
- Spot it in a question: "raw materials", "components", "suppliers", "inbound", "upstream".

**Distribution** (the outbound half of logistics)
- What it is: Getting finished goods out to customers.
- Think of it as: The delivery van and the shops that receive it.
- Spot it in a question: "customers", "outbound", "downstream", "delivery to customers".

**Stationary and movement functions** (physical flow of material)
- What it is: Stationary functions are the places where goods sit: raw materials and packaging inventory, production, work in progress and assembly, finished goods and warehouse, the distribution depot. Movement functions are the moves between them: bulk delivery, transfer, primary transport, local delivery.
- Think of it as: Parking lots (stationary) and the roads between them (movement).
- Spot it in a question: "stationary functions" vs "movement functions". The chart on the same slide shows logistics cost is only one part of the total product cost.

**Logistics information system**
- What it is: The information side of logistics: one central database with four functions around it.
- Think of it as: A metro control room. One board of train positions, with planners, schedulers, announcers and inspectors plugged in.
- Spot it in a question: match the job to the function.
  - Database (centre): external data (customer orders, inbound shipments) and internal data (production, inventory).
  - Planning function: stock management by product, customer and location; demand forecasting; strategy planning.
  - Co-ordination function: production scheduling, materials requirement planning, sales and marketing planning.
  - Customer service communication function: customer order status, inventory availability, outbound shipment status.
  - Control function: customer service levels, vendor performance, carrier performance, system performance.

**ERP** (Enterprise Resource Planning)
- What it is: The company-wide system that holds finance, production, purchasing and inventory records. Its Logistics/SCM module handles supply chain and logistics work.
- Think of it as: The head office ledger. The WMS and TMS are specialist branches that report back to it.
- Spot it in a question: "main transaction system", "back-end corporate systems", "legacy system", "logistics/SCM module".
- The slide places production, sub-assembly, work in progress, packaging and unitisation inside the ERP's logistics module. The WMS covers finished goods inventory and the warehouse. The TMS covers depots and distribution centres.

**SKU** (stock keeping unit)
- What it is: One specific product variant with its own stock code.
- Think of it as: The same shirt in red-M and blue-L are two different SKUs.
- Spot it in a question: "thousands of SKUs", "slotting", "pick face".

**WMS** (Warehouse Management System)
- What it is: Software that controls everything inside a warehouse. It takes purchase orders and customer orders from the ERP, feeds back goods received and dispatched, and gives instructions to equipment.
- Think of it as: A supervisor with a tablet telling each worker and each machine the next step.
- Spot it in a question: any warehouse operation. Why it is needed: a large distribution centre can get thousands of orders a day across thousands of SKUs, with consolidation, packing and dispatch into hundreds of vehicles.

**WMS functions** (one entry each)
- Receiving: yard planning; checks inbound goods against electronic advance shipping notices (ASNs), dimensions, weights and quality sampling. Think: the gate guard checking the delivery against the challan. Spot it: "ASN", "quality sampling".
- Advance shipping notice (ASN): an electronic message sent before the goods arrive, listing what is in the consignment. Think: a "your parcel is coming, here is the packing list" email.
- Put-away: picks the best storage location among all feasible storage types. Think: deciding which shelf a new delivery goes on. Spot it: "best storage location", "storage types".
- Replenishment: refills the pick locations from bulk storage, at a fixed trigger point or after each order. Think: topping up a supermarket's front shelf from the back room. Spot it: "trigger point", "pick locations".
- Picking and slotting: an optimised pick route, and the optimum location of each SKU in the pick face. Think: a supermarket with the fastest sellers at the front. Spot it: "pick route", "slotting".
- Added value services: kitting, labelling and final assembly, which needs a bill of materials (the parts list). Think: a gift-hamper packer. Spot it: "kitting", "final assembly".
- Packing: chooses the correct carton size from a database of SKU dimensions. Think: a small gift box, not a big empty one. Spot it: "carton size".
- Cross-docking: planning, labelling and sortation so goods move straight through. Think: a railway junction where passengers change trains without leaving the platform. Spot it: "straight through".
- Sortation: sorts by order, vehicle or geographical area. Think: a post office sorting letters by pin code. Spot it: "sort by vehicle or area".
- Dispatch: despatch documentation and transmission of ASNs. Think: loading the van and handing over the challan. Spot it: "documentation".
- Management: workload planning, performance measurement, productivity schemes, modelling, billing, pallet management, customs reporting. Think: the manager's dashboard and payroll. Spot it: "billing", "customs reporting".
- Stock counting: full count, or perpetual inventory. A full count counts everything at once. Perpetual inventory counts continuously. Think: a yearly stock-take vs a daily shelf check. Spot it: "perpetual", "full count".
- Batch tracking: tagging stock with its inward batch number so it can be traced. Think: the batch code printed on a medicine strip. Spot it: "inward batch numbers".

**Equipment control system**
- What it is: The system that directs machines (cranes, conveyors, automated guided vehicles, in-line scales, sorters) on the WMS's instructions, and reports feedback and faults.
- Think of it as: A lift operator who takes floor numbers from reception and reports when the lift is stuck.
- Spot it in a question: "directs a crane", "feedback and diagnostics", "mechanical failure", "conveyors, AGVs, in-line scales, sortation".

**AS/RS** (Automated Storage and Retrieval System)
- What it is: Automated racking where cranes move pallets between a pick-up and deposit station and the storage racks.
- Think of it as: A robotic vending machine for pallets.
- Spot it in a question: "crane", "racking", "pallet at the end of the aisle".

**AGV** (Automated Guided Vehicle)
- What it is: A driverless trolley that carries goods along set routes.
- Think of it as: A robot cart that follows a fixed path through the godown.
- Spot it in a question: "automated vehicles" in the equipment list.

**Pick-to-light**
- What it is: Order fulfilment technology. Displays and buttons at storage locations light up to guide a worker to the right item and quantity, and the worker presses a button to record it.
- Think of it as: A restaurant kitchen where a light above each tray tells the waiter which dish to take.
- Spot it in a question: "displays and buttons at storage locations", "guides manual picking". It guides people. It does not capture data.

**Automatic data capture** (how machines read item identity)
- What it is: Reading what an item is without someone typing it in. Two technologies are named: barcode and RFID.

**Barcode**
- What it is: Printed bars read by a scanner that must see the label. It is line-of-sight technology.
- Think of it as: A supermarket scanner that beeps only when the item is held in front of it.
- Spot it in a question: "line of sight".

**RFID** (Radio Frequency Identification)
- What it is: A tag that responds to radio waves. It does not need a line of sight.
- Think of it as: A toll tag (like FASTag) read as the car passes, even behind a windscreen.
- Spot it in a question: "radio wave transmission", "no line of sight", "identify pallets without seeing them".

**TMS** (Transportation Management System)
- What it is: Software for all transport work. The lecture's reference model (Capgemini Consulting, 2011) has three levels, strategic, tactical and operational, plus vehicle and finance areas.
- Think of it as: A company's travel desk for its trucks: which truck, which route, which driver, what it costs.
- Spot it in a question: any transport question about trucks, routes, carriers, loads or fleets.

**TMS strategic functions** (five)
- Transport sourcing; performance management (using past data, business intelligence); network design; carbon footprint calculations (CO2 from the distribution network); contract management.
- Think of it as: Long-term decisions about which carriers to hire, how the network is laid out and how green it is.
- Spot it in a question: "sourcing", "network design", "carbon footprint", "contracts with carriers".

**TMS tactical functions** (four)
- Capacity management: forecasting and simulation so enough capacity is ready when needed. Asset and fleet management: vehicles, containers and pallets, plus financing, maintenance, telematics, fuel and health and safety. Resource management: the company's own staff and hired staff. Rate and tariff management: carrier rates for customers and subcontracted carriers, across borders, with tariff and tax.
- Think of it as: Planning how many trucks and drivers the season needs, and which rate card applies.
- Spot it in a question: "enough capacity when needed" = capacity. "maintenance, fuel, financing" = asset and fleet. "employees" = resource. "rates and tariffs" = rate and tariff.

**TMS operational planning** (five)
- Shipping consolidation and load or trip planning (which goods go in which trip). Load design (the three-dimensional arrangement inside the truck). Route planning (the actual street-level route). Fleet and driver planning (which own vehicles and drivers go on which loads, within driving-hour, location and equipment limits). Carrier selection (the transport mode and the actual carrier).
- Spot it in a question: "inside the truck, three dimensions" = load design. "street-level path" = route planning. "driving hours" = fleet and driver planning. "choose the carrier or mode" = carrier selection.

**TMS operational execution** (seven)
- Order entry and consolidation; dispatching (informing carriers or own drivers); order status information (pick-up and delivery records); global logistic execution with customs and transport documents; yard management (yard and dock doors); parcel management (express parcels); execution monitoring (visibility, traceability and corrective action).
- Spot it in a question: "dispatch", "customs", "yard", "parcel", "monitor". Dispatching is execution, not planning.

**TMS vehicle and finance areas**
- Vehicle: vehicle management, route planning and communication. Finance: carrier invoice settlement, customer billing, freight bill auditing, transport cost allocation, time and labour.
- Spot it in a question: "billing" or "freight bill audit" points to finance. "vehicle management" points to vehicle.

### 5. E-commerce supply chain

**Distribution system: channel and logistics**
- What it is: The distribution system has two halves. The distribution channel answers "who sells the product". Distribution logistics answers "how the goods are stored, moved and serviced".
- Think of it as: Every shop answers two questions: who is selling, and how does the product reach the buyer.
- Spot it in a question: "broker", "subsidiary", "wholesale", "retail trade", "franchising", "infomediary" = channel. "warehouse", "roads", "waterways", "delivery service" = logistics.

**Direct and indirect outlets** (distribution channel)
- What it is: A direct outlet is sold by the provider itself, through a broker, a subsidiary, electronic automation or its own e-shop. An indirect outlet goes through wholesale trade, retail trade, franchising or infomediaries.
- Think of it as: Selling your own mangoes from your cart (direct), vs selling through the mandi, a wholesaler and a shop (indirect).
- Spot it in a question: "intermediary" or "wholesaler" points to indirect. "own e-shop" points to direct.

**Physical and digital components of distribution**
- What it is: Some distribution items are physical: brokers, subsidiaries, wholesale, retail, franchising, warehouses, roads, waterways, delivery service. Others are digital: electronic automation, e-shop, infomediaries, digital libraries, electronic communications networks, remote diagnosis or repair.
- Think of it as: Two columns in a ledger: things you can touch, and things you only download or switch on.
- Spot it in a question: "digital library", "e-shop", "remote diagnosis" = digital components.

**Online distribution of digital products**
- What it is: The product is digital (music, e-books, software, documents) and travels over a digital transmission medium instead of by lorry. Two cases: direct (provider to customer) and indirect (provider, then intermediary, then customer).
- Think of it as: Downloading a film from the studio's own site, or from a streaming middleman.
- Spot it in a question: "digital product", "digital transmission medium", "no physical transport".

**Advantages of online distribution** (five)
- The customer's wish to buy can be met immediately and at any time. The seller has direct customer contact. No bottlenecks in reproducing digital goods, and no long delivery times. Lower production, storage and distribution costs mean price and cost advantages. Niche products with low circulation can be sold economically.
- Memory: Instant, Direct, No bottleneck, Cheaper, Niche.

**Disadvantages of online distribution** (six)
- Digital products can be copied and distributed illegally (security issues, digital watermarks). Customers need access to a digital network. Customers' search, decision and purchase behaviour is recorded, which leads to target marketing. Distribution costs are in most cases passed on to the customer. There is no social or interpersonal contact, which decreases loyalty. Compressed digital goods lose quality.
- Memory: Copied, Connected, Captured, Charged, Cold, Compressed.

**Offline distribution**
- What it is: Physical goods travel the classic way: provider, storage, transport, point of delivery, customer. Storage has concerns about temperature, place, number and space. Transport has concerns about time demand and route optimisation. Point of delivery covers delivery or collection, and the time of hand-over.
- Think of it as: The kirana supply line: godown, delivery van, doorstep hand-over.
- Spot it in a question: "physical product", "storage", "transport route", "point of delivery".

**Hybrid distribution**
- What it is: A physical product with an auxiliary digital part, for example a gadget with an app or a book with online access. The provider talks to an intermediary over a digital channel. The intermediary handles storage, transport and delivery of the physical part. The digital part reaches the customer electronically.
- Think of it as: A printed textbook that comes with a free online access code.
- Spot it in a question: "physical product" together with "auxiliary digital part".

**Distribution network design options** (six, from Chopra, 2003)
- What it is: Six ways to decide where the stock sits and how it reaches the customer.
- Think of it as: Who holds the stock, and who gets it to the customer.
- Spot it in a question: match the scenario to the option.
  - Manufacturer storage with direct shipping: the product goes from the maker straight to the end customer, bypassing the retailer. The retailer may take the order and trigger the request. Think: dropshipping. The slides give Dell and online retailers that dropship as examples.
  - Manufacturer storage with direct shipping and in-transit merge: pieces of an order coming from different locations are combined on the way, so the customer gets one delivery.
  - Distributor storage with package carrier delivery: stock sits in intermediate warehouses, and package carriers (outside courier companies) take it to the final customer.
  - Distributor storage with last mile delivery: the distributor or retailer delivers to the customer's home itself. The slide says this suits fast-moving and grocery items.
  - Manufacturer or distributor storage with customer pickup: stock sits in a warehouse. Customers order online or by phone and collect at designated pickup points.
  - Retail storage with customer pickup: stock sits in local stores. Customers walk in, or order online or by phone, and collect at the store.
- Memory: options 1 and 2 hold stock at the maker. Options 3 and 4 hold it at a distributor. Options 5 and 6 leave the customer to come and collect.

**Package carrier**
- What it is: An outside courier or parcel company that moves packages to the customer.
- Think of it as: The courier on the doorstep, not the shop's own bike.
- Spot it in a question: "package carrier" vs "the retailer delivers to the home itself".

**In-transit merge**
- What it is: Pieces of one customer's order, picked up from different places, are combined in transit so they arrive as one delivery.
- Think of it as: A family's shopping bought from three shops that reaches the same door in one trip.
- Spot it in a question: "combines pieces from different locations", "single delivery".

**On-demand production**
- What it is: Products are built after a concrete customer order. The aim is mass production's low cost combined with order production's fit for the individual customer (mass customisation).
- Think of it as: A laptop you configure online, built after you order, and priced close to the standard model.
- Spot it in a question: "based on concrete customer orders", "mass customisation", "cost leadership with customer orientation".

**Value chain with on-demand production** (slide 109)
- What it is: A company-wide top layer (company and financial planning, personnel development, research and development, use of information and communication technologies). Below it, a row of customised steps: survey of customer requests, customised production, customised procurement, customised manufacturing and assembly, customised distribution, customer-related service.
- Think of it as: A wedding planner's checklist. The budget and staff are fixed at the top, and every step below is tailored to the couple.
- Spot it in a question: the word "customised" on a step. The top layer is always company-wide.

**Order-neutral parts** (pre-fabrication)
- What it is: Standard parts made before any order arrives, because they are not yet specific to one customer. The final shaping happens after the order.
- Think of it as: Pre-made paneer blocks that become a specific dish only when you order.
- Spot it in a question: "pre-fabrication of order-neutral parts".

**Modelling, simulation and visualisation tools**
- What it is: Tools that let the customer compare product configurations and price models, using electronic product catalogues, before ordering.
- Think of it as: A car website where you change the colour and wheels and watch the car update.
- Spot it in a question: "compare configurations", "electronic product catalogues", "simulate".

**Order tracking system**
- What it is: Lets the customer follow the order from production through to delivery.
- Think of it as: The live tracking map on a food delivery app.
- Spot it in a question: "follow the completion process up to delivery". Tracking is also a facility for online buyers, so read the context.

**Multichannel management**
- What it is: Keeping the customer dialogue going through several channels (web, phone, store) with one consistent view.
- Think of it as: A bank giving the same answer whether you phone, visit a branch or use the app.
- Spot it in a question: "multichannel", "customer dialogue through different channels".

**Customer data warehouse**
- What it is: A central store of customer data. It updates each customer's profile, computes the customer's current value, and supports the whole value chain.
- Think of it as: A shared customer diary that every department reads before it calls the customer.
- Spot it in a question: "customer profile", "current customer value", "maintains relationships for the entire value chain".

## How it flows

**AIDA ladder (the internet is the channel that carries it)**
```text
Attention ──► Interest ──► Desire ──► Action
(stranger    (shows the   (suits my   (buys)
 notices)     benefits)    need)
```
Each step is one job for the marketer. A question usually describes one step.

**Marketing paradigm shift**
```text
Mass ──► Differentiated ──► Segment ──► Niche ──► Individual ──► Mass customised
1940-50   1950-60           1960-70    1970-80    1980-90        1990 onwards
```
Read left to right: from "one message for all" to "a product for each person, at mass cost".

**Online customer model**
```text
Online surfer ──► Online consumer ──► Online prosumer ──► Online buyer ──► Key online customer
(browses)         (bookmarks,         (co-designs,        (buys)           (returns, CRM)
                   reads, downloads)   shares info)
```
Each stage needs its own facility. The surfer needs a domain name; the consumer needs view-and-feel tools; the prosumer needs tailoring; the buyer needs a cart and payment; the key customer needs CRM and offers.

**Push vs pull**
```text
PUSH:  Provider ──────────────► Customer     (provider starts it; spam risk; subscribe/unsubscribe)
PULL:  Customer ──► asks ──► Provider       (user starts it; user may pay a fee)
```

**Supply chain and its two flows**
```text
Supplier's supplier ──► Supplier ──► The firm ──► Distributor ──► Customer
                    ◄── ORDERS flow upstream ◄──────────────────────────
                    ─── DELIVERIES flow downstream ─────────────────────►
```
Orders go up the chain (upstream). Deliveries go down it (downstream).

**The four cycles**
```text
Customer ──[customer buying cycle]── Dealer ──[replenishment cycle]── Distributor
   ──[manufacturing cycle]── Manufacturer ──[procurement cycle]── Supplier
```
Each cycle is the loop between two neighbouring links. Name the two links, and you have named the cycle.

**SCOR model**
```text
PLAN  (forecasts and opportunities, sits above the others)
SOURCE ──► MAKE ──► DELIVER
(buy)      (produce)  (ship to the customer)
```

**Purchaser and supplier documents**
```text
Purchaser ──► Supplier : RFQ, purchase order, PO change, receiving notice, payment advice
Purchaser ◄── Supplier : RFQ response, PO acknowledgement, status response, shipping notice, invoice
```

**B2B payment flow**
```text
Purchaser ──► Purchaser's bank : payment authorisation
Purchaser's bank ──► Supplier's bank : electronic transfer of funds (with remittance notice)
Supplier's bank ──► Supplier : deposit notice
Purchaser's bank ──► Purchaser : payment remittance notice
```

**Bullwhip effect**
```text
Customer sales ──► Retailer orders ──► Wholesaler orders ──► Distributor orders ──► Factory
(small swings)     (bigger)            (bigger still)        (bigger)              (biggest swings)
```
Each link adds its own guess and its own batch, so the swing grows as you go upstream.

**Information sharing: before and after**
```text
Without sharing:  Customer ──► Retailer ──► Wholesaler ──► Factory     (each link guesses)
With sharing:     Customer ──► every partner sees the same real demand
```

                              NO                     YES

## Don't mix these up

| These look alike | How to tell them apart | Tie-breaker word |
|---|---|---|
| Online surfer vs online consumer | Surfer browses aimlessly. Consumer has goal-directed, repeated contact and bookmarks the site | "bookmark" or "repeated" → consumer |
| Online consumer vs online prosumer | Consumer views, reads and downloads. Prosumer also tailors the product and shares personal information | "tailor" or "share personal information" → prosumer |
| Online buyer vs key online customer | Buyer has made a purchase online. Key customer returns for multiple purchases and gets CRM | "returning" or "multiple" → key customer |
| Push vs pull | Push: the provider starts it. Pull: the user starts it | Who starts it |
| Spam vs customised push | Both are provider-started. Spam is unwanted mass mail. Customised push is tailored to the person | "tailored" → customised push; "unwanted, no way out" → spam |
| Pull principle vs context targeting | Pull is about who starts the demand. Context is about where an ad shows, for someone already searching | "user requests and pays" → pull; "already searching now" → context |
| Nanosite vs microsite | Nanosite opens inside the current page. Microsite is a separate, specially designed site | "without leaving the current page" → nanosite |
| Microsite vs branded content | Microsite stands alone. Branded content is a section of the company's own site | "part of the website" → branded content |
| Banner advertising vs embedded content | Banner is an ad placed on a page. Embedded content sits inside the content (keyword search, promoted news) | "keyword search" or "promoted news" → embedded |
| Community vs content vs context targeting | Community: virtual community sites. Content: related websites of other organisations. Context: people searching the subject now | "virtual community", "related website", "currently searching" |
| Demographic/psychographic vs online behaviour | Who the person is vs what they did online | "cookies" or "browsing" → online behaviour |
| Explicit vs implicit data | Explicit: the customer gives it. Implicit: observed from behaviour | "clicks", "never provided" → implicit |
| Structured vs unstructured data | Structured: fixed rows and columns. Unstructured: free text and media | "database, fields" vs "reviews, free text" |
| Interactive marketing vs interactive design | Marketing is two-way talk. Design is the product page the customer configures | "chat, forms, questionnaires" → marketing |
| Sales force automation vs sales management | Automation captures sales data in real time. Management gives decision support to managers | "decision support" → sales management |
| B2C vs B2B | Business to consumer (individual buyers) vs business to business (company buyers) | "individual consumer" vs "another company" |
| Mass marketing vs mass customised marketing | Same product for everyone vs personalised at mass cost | "same for everyone" vs "each customer, at mass cost" |
| Value chain vs supply chain | Value chain: steps inside one firm. Supply chain: many firms from supplier to customer | "inside one firm" → value chain |
| Supply chain vs SCM | Supply chain is the network. SCM is managing that network | "system of organisations" vs "managing, coordinating" |
| Logistics vs supply chain | Logistics moves and stores goods. Supply chain is the whole network of partners | "moving goods" vs "all partners" |
| Materials management vs distribution | Materials management brings inputs in. Distribution sends finished goods out | "raw materials, components" → materials; "customers" → distribution |
| Upstream vs downstream | Upstream is toward suppliers. Downstream is toward customers | "supplier" or "customer" side |
| Integration vs interoperability | Integration is the deeper, planned tie-up. Interoperability is the ability to exchange and use data | "always" or "necessarily": integrated implies interoperable, not the reverse |
| Technical vs semantic interoperability | Technical: the systems can connect. Semantic: they mean the same thing | "meaning differs but data transfers" → semantic |
| Data vs service interoperability | Data: exchanging database content (EDI, XML). Service: running separately built applications (CORBA, SOA) | "databases, messages" → data; "applications, architecture" → service |
| Information integration vs collaboration | Information integration is technical links. Collaboration is managerial (contracts, joint decisions) | "contract" or "joint decision" → collaboration |
| Planning vs inventory collaboration | Planning: what, when, where and how to produce. Inventory: when to order and how much | "production" vs "order quantity" |
| Type 0 vs Type 1 | Type 1 shares forecasts and action plans. Type 0 shares nothing | "exchange demand information" → Type 1 |
| Type 1 (CPFR) vs Type 2 (VMI) | Type 1: both still order on their own. Type 2: the supplier generates the order and carries the stock responsibility | "supplier generates the replenishment order" → VMI |
| Type 2 (VMI) vs Type 3 (synchronised) | VMI: inventory only. Synchronised: planning and inventory, with replenishment merged into the supplier's material planning | "merged with material planning" → synchronised |
| Consignment stock vs ordinary stock | Consignment stays the supplier's until used. Ordinary stock is bought | "pays when used" → consignment |
| Bullwhip effect vs variability | Variability is the general swing. Bullwhip is the growth of that swing as you go upstream | "travel up the chain" → bullwhip |
| Cause vs fix (variability) | Causes: forecasting, lead time, batching, price changes, inflated orders, lack of central information. Fixes: sharing information | "lack of centralised information" is a cause; "centralised information sharing" is a fix |
| Bullwhip cause vs consequence | Causes are the factors that create swings. Consequences are safety stock, poor service, wasted resources, transport cost | "named as a factor" → cause; "extra cost or stock" → consequence |
| Order vs delivery vs production lead time | Order lead time: the order travelling up. Delivery lead time: the goods travelling down. Production lead time: the factory making goods | "order placed", "delivered", "made at the factory" |
| Primary vs support activities | Primary activities move value to the customer. Support activities run in the background | "procurement, HR, IT, infrastructure" → support |
| Manufacturing cycle vs procurement cycle | Manufacturing: distributor and manufacturer loop (production options). Procurement: manufacturer and supplier loop (buying raw materials) | "on-demand, just-in-time" vs "raw materials, sell side, buy side" |
| SCOR "Source" vs procurement cycle | Source is a SCOR category. Procurement is a loop in the four cycles. Same idea, different framework | Use the framework the question names |
| Customer buying cycle vs replenishment cycle | Customer buying: customer and dealer. Replenishment: dealer and distributor | "after-sales support" vs "restock the shop" |
| Sell side vs buy side vs marketplace | Sell side: one supplier's catalogue to many buyers. Buy side: the buyer runs the buying. Marketplace: many buyers and sellers meet | "supplier to many buyers" → sell side; "many sellers compete" → marketplace |
| RFQ vs purchase order vs invoice | RFQ asks for prices. PO orders goods. Invoice bills for goods supplied | "price", "order", "bill" |
| Shipping notice vs receiving notice | Shipping notice: supplier says goods left. Receiving notice: buyer says goods arrived | "left" vs "arrived" |
| Payment advice vs remittance notice vs deposit notice | Advice: buyer says payment is coming. Remittance: what the payment covers. Deposit: supplier's bank says money arrived | "coming", "covers", "credited" |
| Payment authorisation vs electronic transfer of funds | Authorisation: purchaser tells its own bank to pay. Transfer: bank-to-bank money movement | "purchaser to own bank" vs "bank to bank" |
| Logistics vs logistics information system | Logistics moves goods. The information system is the database and functions that track them | "database, planning, control" → information system |
| ERP vs WMS vs TMS | ERP: whole-company records. WMS: warehouse. TMS: transport | "company-wide" → ERP; "shelves, bins, pallets" → WMS; "trucks, routes, carriers" → TMS |
| WMS vs equipment control system | WMS decides and instructs. Equipment control moves machines and reports faults | "crane", "conveyor", "mechanical failure" → equipment control |
| Put-away vs replenishment vs picking | Put-away: new goods into storage. Replenishment: refill pick locations from bulk. Picking: take items for an order | "new arrivals" / "refill the front shelves" / "fill an order" |
| Cross-docking vs sortation | Cross-docking: goods pass straight through. Sortation: groups goods by order, vehicle or area | "no storage" → cross-docking; "by vehicle or area" → sortation |
| Receiving vs dispatch | Receiving: goods in, checked against ASNs. Dispatch: goods out, with documents | "in" vs "out"; "dimensions and weights" → receiving |
| Full count vs perpetual inventory | Full count: everything at once. Perpetual: continuous | "always up to date" → perpetual |
| Barcode vs RFID | Barcode needs line of sight. RFID uses radio waves | "line of sight" → barcode; "radio, no line of sight" → RFID |
| Pick-to-light vs barcode | Pick-to-light guides a person with lights. Barcode captures data | "lights, displays, buttons" → pick-to-light |
| AS/RS vs AGV | AS/RS: cranes and fixed racking. AGV: a driverless vehicle on set routes | "crane and racking" vs "driverless vehicle" |
| TMS strategic vs tactical vs operational | Strategic: long-term network and contracts. Tactical: medium-term capacity and rates. Operational: daily trips and execution | "network design, contracts" → strategic; "capacity forecast" → tactical; "dispatch, route" → operational |
| Capacity management vs resource management | Capacity: trucks and transport capacity. Resource: people (own and hired staff) | "vehicles, capacity" vs "employees" |
| Asset and fleet management vs fleet and driver planning | Asset and fleet: maintenance, financing, fuel for owned assets. Fleet and driver planning: assigning vehicles and drivers to loads | "maintenance" vs "assign to loads" |
| Carrier selection vs fleet and driver planning | Carrier selection picks the mode or an outside carrier. Fleet and driver planning uses own equipment and drivers | "own equipment, driving hours" → fleet and driver planning |
| Transport sourcing vs carrier selection | Sourcing is the strategic buying of transport services. Carrier selection is for one shipment | "contract", "strategic" vs "this load" |
| Load design vs route planning vs load/trip planning | Load design: 3D arrangement inside the truck. Route: the street path. Load/trip: which goods share a trip | "three dimensions" / "street level" / "which goods together" |
| Order entry vs dispatching | Order entry records and validates orders. Dispatching informs carriers or drivers | "inform the carrier" → dispatching |
| Distribution channel vs distribution logistics | Channel: who sells. Logistics: how goods are stored, moved and serviced | "broker, wholesale, franchising" vs "warehouse, transport, service" |
| Direct outlet vs indirect outlet | Direct: the provider sells itself. Indirect: through wholesale, retail, franchise or infomediary | "intermediary" → indirect |
| Online vs offline vs hybrid distribution | Online: all digital. Offline: all physical. Hybrid: a physical product with a digital part | "digital part of a physical product" → hybrid |
| Direct shipping vs in-transit merge | Direct shipping sends from the maker to the customer. In-transit merge combines pieces into one delivery | "single delivery from different places" → merge |
| Package carrier vs last-mile delivery | Package carrier is an outside courier. Last-mile is the distributor's own delivery to the home | "package carrier" vs "delivers to the home itself" |
| Manufacturer/distributor pickup vs retail pickup | Where the stock sits: warehouse vs local store | "local retail stores" → retail pickup |
| On-demand production vs just-in-time | On-demand starts after a customer order. JIT means supplies arrive exactly when needed | "customer order" → on-demand; "exactly when needed" → JIT |
| Mass production vs on-demand production | Mass: one standard product in bulk. On-demand: built per order, at near mass cost | "configured for the customer" → on-demand |

## How this week gets asked

**The question patterns**
- Scenario, then "which concept, model, stage or cycle?" This is the most common shape. A short story (a website, a shop, a warehouse, a truck, a supplier) is followed by four official names.
- "Which is NOT ..." questions. Cross out the items that fit, and the leftover one is the answer. A "NOT" item is often a real fact from the lecture placed in the wrong list (for example, a process step from a different cycle).
- "Which benefit, advantage or consequence." The right option is positive. The wrong options flip the sign: higher cost, slower time, smaller reach, more stock.
- "Which dimension, level or type." Interoperability (data or service, technical or semantic), SCM objective levels (strategic, tactical, operational), collaboration Types 0 to 3.
- "Which WMS or TMS function does this." Read the verb in the scenario: receive, put away, refill, pick, pack, sort, dispatch. For transport: plan, route, load, assign, dispatch, monitor.
- "Which technology captures the data." Line of sight (barcode) vs radio (RFID). Guiding lights (pick-to-light) is a separate option.
- "Which data type, targeting method or promotion principle." Explicit vs implicit, context vs community, push vs pull.
- Definition match. The option that paraphrases the definition (for example, disintermediation, bullwhip, supply chain) is correct.
- Cure vs cause. The question asks what fixes a problem, and the wrong options list the causes.

**Distractor traps for this week**
- Absolute words: "always", "never", "only", "eliminates", "identical", "without exception". Check them against the lecture. Integration implies interoperability, but not the other way round. Information sharing reduces variability; it does not eliminate it.
- Causes disguised as fixes. Batch ordering, inflated orders and lack of centralised information are causes of variability. Sharing information is the fix.
- Right concept, wrong stage or cycle. Procurement is never in the customer buying cycle. Manufacturing options (on-demand, just-in-time) are not procurement strategies.
- Near-twins. Nanosite vs microsite, push vs pull, CPFR vs VMI vs synchronised, barcode vs RFID, distributor with package carrier vs distributor with last mile, strategic vs tactical vs operational.
- Buyer facilities offered as prosumer answers. A shopping cart, payment or delivery tracking is a buyer-stage facility, not the step up to prosumer.
- True statements about a different level. "Network design" is a real SCM outcome, but it belongs to the strategic level, not the tactical one.
- Invented-sounding terms. Phrases such as "lead-time compression" or "demand synchronisation" are not in the lecture. If an option uses a term that is not on this week's lists, it is usually the wrong one.

**If the question says ___ → pick the option about ___**
1. "Without leaving the current page" → nanosite.
2. "Unsolicited", "no way to stop", "cannot unsubscribe" → the push principle, with its subscribe and unsubscribe facility as the missing piece.
3. "Already searching for information on this subject" → context targeting.
4. "Variability grows as we travel up the chain" or "factory orders swing far more than sales" → bullwhip effect.
5. "Supplier generates the replenishment order and keeps the retailer's stock level" → VMI (Type 2). If the supplier merges replenishment with its own material planning instead → synchronised supply chain (Type 3).
6. "Order independently but exchange demand information and action plans" → collaborative planning and forecasting (Type 1).
7. "Crane", "conveyor", "mechanical failure" → equipment control system. "Best storage location for a received item" → put-away.
8. "Radio waves, no line of sight" → RFID. "Line of sight" → barcode.
9. "Applications designed separately must work together" → service interoperability. "Different databases exchanging information, EDI or XML" → data interoperability.
10. "Physical product with an auxiliary digital part" → hybrid distribution.
11. "Customers walk into the store or order online and collect there" → retail storage with customer pickup.

## Practice walk-through

**Q1.** A company builds a separate website for a new product line instead of using its main corporate site. Which online-surfer strategy is this?
- Answer: A) Independent domain for marketing
- Why: Choosing a separate domain for marketing is the domain name strategy under online surfers.
- Trap: "Community-based targeting" sounds like a marketing move, but it is an ad-targeting component, not a domain decision.

**Q2.** Which facility is most important for turning an online consumer into an online prosumer?
- Answer: B) Product personalization and visualization tools
- Why: Prosumers want tailored products and a way to see the effect. This is the prosumer facility list.
- Trap: "Shopping cart and payment gateway" is a buyer-stage facility, so it moves a customer to buying, not to co-designing.

**Q3.** An online retailer analyses browsing logs captured through cookies to decide future advertising. The targeting approach relies mainly on:
- Answer: D) Online behaviour information
- Why: Cookies capture browsing behaviour, which is online behaviour targeting.
- Trap: "Contextual information" sounds like browsing, but context targeting is about people searching a subject right now.

**Q4.** A manufacturer buys raw materials through a digital marketplace where several suppliers compete. Which cycle is this?
- Answer: A) Procurement cycle
- Why: Buying raw materials from suppliers is the manufacturer-supplier loop, and the marketplace is a procurement strategy.
- Trap: "Manufacturing cycle" fits the manufacturer, but that cycle covers production options (on-demand and just-in-time).

**Q5.** Which outcome is most closely tied to the tactical level of SCM decision making?
- Answer: C) Inventory target determination
- Why: Tactical outcomes are the demand forecast, the production, procurement and logistics plan, and inventory targets.
- Trap: "Network design" is a real SCM outcome, but it belongs to the strategic level.

**Q6.** A company uses XML-based messages to exchange inventory data with suppliers, even though their database platforms differ. This mainly addresses:
- Answer: B) Data interoperability
- Why: Exchanging information between different databases is data interoperability. EDI and XML are the standards named for it.
- Trap: "Service interoperability" concerns separately built applications, not exchanged data.

**Q7.** Which statement best illustrates the relationship between integration and interoperability?
- Answer: C) Integrated systems are necessarily interoperable, but not vice versa.
- Why: Every integrated pair can exchange and use data, but an interoperable pair is not necessarily integrated.
- Trap: "Integration and interoperability are identical concepts" sounds neat, but they are different ideas.

**Q8.** A retailer and supplier jointly decide replenishment quantities, but the supplier does not receive production planning information. Which model is most consistent with this?
- Answer: C) Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI)
- Why: VMI gives replenishment to the supplier, uses consignment stock, and the lecture lists that the supplier may not share production planning information.
- Trap: "Synchronized supply chain" also covers replenishment, but it merges the supplier's material planning, which this scenario rules out.

**Q9.** A transport planner allocates drivers to routes while respecting driving-hour limits and vehicle availability. This activity belongs to:
- Answer: B) Fleet and driver planning
- Why: Fleet and driver planning assigns own vehicles and drivers to loads and routes, within limits like driving hours and equipment condition.
- Trap: "Route planning" fits because routes are mentioned, but it plans the street path, not who drives.

**Q10.** A customer orders a desktop computer online. The monitor ships from one location and the CPU from another, but both arrive together in one delivery. Which distribution design is used?
- Answer: C) Manufacturer storage with direct shipping and in-transit merge
- Why: In-transit merge combines pieces from different locations into a single delivery.
- Trap: "Manufacturer storage with direct shipping" ships from the maker, but it does not merge pieces in transit.

## 60-second recap

- E-marketing is the AIDA steps run through the internet: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action.
- The target era is mass customised marketing (1990 onwards): personal feel at mass cost.
- The online customer ladder is surfer, consumer, prosumer, buyer, key customer. Each stage has its own facility.
- Push means the provider starts it, and it needs a subscribe and unsubscribe option. Pull means the user starts it.
- Targeting: community, content and context, plus demographic/psychographic and online behaviour (cookies).
- The four Ps are Product, Price, Place and Promotion.
- Four cycles: customer buying, replenishment, manufacturing, procurement. Procurement strategies: sell side, buy side, marketplace.
- Orders travel upstream and deliveries travel downstream. SCOR is Plan, Source, Make, Deliver.
- Bullwhip effect: variability grows as you go up the chain. The fix is information sharing.
- Integration is collaborating and sharing. Interoperability is exchanging and using data, at technical and semantic levels.
- Collaboration Types: 0 nothing, 1 planning (collaborative planning and forecasting), 2 inventory (VMI), 3 both (synchronised).
- VMI: the supplier generates the replenishment order and carries the stock responsibility, using consignment stock. Watch the four integration issues and three flows.
- WMS runs the warehouse: receive, put away, refill, pick, pack, cross-dock, sort, dispatch. Equipment control runs the machines.
- Barcode needs line of sight. RFID uses radio. Pick-to-light guides the operator.
- TMS has strategic, tactical and operational levels. Load design, route planning, fleet and driver planning and carrier selection are operational planning.
- Digital goods travel online. Physical goods travel offline. Hybrid is a physical product with a digital part.
- Six distribution network options: stock at the maker (1-2), at a distributor (3-4), or collected by the customer (5-6).
- On-demand production builds after the customer orders, at close to mass-production cost.
