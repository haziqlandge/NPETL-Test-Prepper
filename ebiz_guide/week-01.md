# Week 1 — Introduction to E-Business

## In one breath

A business exists to earn money by giving customers something they value, so that they keep coming back. E-commerce is the buying-and-selling part of that, done over computer networks. E-business is the bigger idea: every business process, from buying stock to answering a complaint, runs on automated systems, mostly on the web. This week also covers who-sells-to-whom deals, the nine ways a website earns money, the Gartner hype cycle (why many internet firms crashed), and two Indian case studies: Tata Steel buying online and ITC's eChoupal village kiosks.

**The big analogy:** a kirana store (a small neighbourhood grocery shop in India). Taking orders on WhatsApp or by phone is e-commerce. Running the whole shop on connected systems, ordering stock online, tracking deliveries, keeping one set of accounts and sharing sales data with the brand, is e-business. The ways the shop makes money (margin on goods, a delivery fee, a membership, a brand paying for a poster on the shop board) are its business models.

## Concept cards

### Business, commerce and e-business

**Business**
- What it is: an organisation that supplies goods or services to customers and needs enough paying customers to make a profit. Businesses can be privately owned, not-for-profit or state-owned.
- Think of it as: the kirana shop. It exists so that people come in, pay, and the money left after costs is the profit.
- Spot it in a question: "organisation", "goods or services", "customers", "profit", "revenue". The right answer talks about value or profit, not about the building.

**Aaker's definition of a business**
- What it is: a business is generally an organisational unit that has (or should have) a defined strategy and a manager with sales and profit responsibility. Source on slide 12: David A. Aaker and Damien McLoughlin, *Strategic Market Management*.
- Think of it as: a shop with a clear plan (what to sell, to whom) and one named person answerable for sales and profit.
- Spot it in a question: "strategic view", "who is responsible for profit", "no clear revenue model". The answer is "a defined strategy and profit responsibility" (Bank Week 1 Q1, Mock 2 Q4).

**Purpose of a business**
- What it is: to generate revenue, by delivering value to the customer and satisfying them (slide 13).
- Think of it as: a restaurant that earns only if diners leave happy and pay.
- Spot it in a question: "why does a business exist" → "generate revenue, deliver value, satisfy customers".

**Oxford and Wikipedia definitions of business**
- What it is: the Oxford English Dictionary gives eight meanings, including "a commercial organization" and "commercial activity". Wikipedia calls business "the social science of managing people to organize and maintain collective productivity... usually to generate revenue".
- Think of it as: the shop, the trade, and the people who run it, all under one word.
- Spot it in a question: definitions quoted from a dictionary or Wikipedia. The exam uses the "organisation that earns revenue from customers" idea.

**High-performance business (four building blocks, slide 17)**
- What it is: set strategies to satisfy key stakeholders by improving critical business processes and aligning resources and organisation. The four blocks are:
  - Stakeholders: customers, employees, suppliers, distributors, stockholders, financial institutions, government.
  - Processes: work flows that link departments (for example sales, shipping, installation, maintenance, support).
  - Resources: labour, material, machines, information, energy.
  - Organisation: structures, policies, corporate cultures.
- Think of it as: a cricket team. Stakeholders are the fans, sponsors and board; processes are the planned plays; resources are the bats, balls and players; organisation is the coach, the rulebook and the team culture.
- Spot it in a question: "policies, structures, culture" → organisation block. "work flows linking departments" → processes block.

**Commerce**
- What it is: the activity of buying and selling, especially on a large scale (Oxford English Dictionary, slide 18).
- Think of it as: the till and the bargaining at the counter.
- Spot it in a question: "buying and selling", "trade". Commerce is a subset of business. All businesses involve some commercial activity, but business also covers making things, managing people and organising work.

**E-commerce**
- What it is: buying, selling, marketing and servicing products or services over computer networks. It uses the Internet and the Web and may include electronic payment systems (slides 21 and 22).
- Think of it as: taking WhatsApp orders from customers and accepting UPI payment for the goods. The selling step only.
- Spot it in a question: "buying and selling goods over a digital network", "electronic payments". If the option talks about internal records, partners or servicing only, it is e-business, not e-commerce.

**E-business**
- What it is: any business process that relies on an automated information system, mostly web-based today (slide 22). It covers the whole value chain: electronic purchasing and supply chain management, processing orders electronically, handling customer service, and cooperating with business partners. The lecture's summary: "in general, conducting all business activities online". The term was coined by Lou Gerstner, CEO of IBM (International Business Machines).
- Think of it as: the whole kirana shop running on a connected system, not just the till.
- Spot it in a question: "supporting internal transactions", "collaborating with partners", "all business activities online" → e-business. A wrong option might say "e-business is only buying and selling".
- Quote to know: Andy Grove, Intel chairman, in 1999 said that all companies would become Internet companies or die (slide 9).

### Customer value and satisfaction

**Customer delivered value (Kotler)**
- What it is: total customer value minus total customer cost. It is the net gain a customer feels (slide 14).
- Think of it as: a bargain. What you get, minus what it costs you in money, time, effort and hassle.
- Spot it in a question: "free onboarding, faster support, simpler return process" → the thing that goes up is customer delivered value (Bank Week 1 Q2).

**Total customer value**
- What it is: the bundle of benefits customers expect from a product or service.
- Think of it as: the whole basket of benefits: the phone's camera, battery life, service centre, brand name.
- Spot it in a question: "bundle of benefits" → total customer value. Not the same as the value gap.

**Total customer cost**
- What it is: the bundle of costs a customer expects to incur in evaluating, obtaining, using and disposing of a product or service.
- Think of it as: price, plus time spent searching, plus the wait for delivery, plus the effort of returning it.
- Spot it in a question: "costs", "hassle", "disposing". A good onboarding or simpler returns process lowers this cost; it does not raise it.

**Customer satisfaction**
- What it is: a person's feeling of pleasure or disappointment that results from comparing a product's perceived performance (or outcome) with his or her expectation (slide 15).
- Think of it as: the feeling when a Swiggy order arrives. Happy if the food beat what you expected, disappointed if it did not.
- Spot it in a question: "feeling", "pleasure or disappointment", "compared with expectation" → satisfaction (Mock 3 Q2). A wrong option will name customer delivered value instead.

**Customer loyalty**
- What it is: satisfied customers are loyal customers. The lecture's key point: high loyalty comes from delivering high customer value (slide 15).
- Think of it as: a regular at the tea stall who never switches because the tea is always good value.
- Spot it in a question: "key to generating high customer loyalty" → "delivering high customer value" (Mock 2 Q3). Lowering prices on everything is a trap; the lecture does not say that.

**Value proposition and value-delivery system**
- What it is: a company must develop a competitively superior value proposition (the reason to buy from it) and a superior value-delivery system (the way it reliably delivers that promise). Slide 15.
- Think of it as: the shop board that says "fresh vegetables delivered by 8 am, 10% cheaper". The promise is the proposition; the delivery boy and the timing are the delivery system.
- Spot it in a question: "competitively superior" plus "promise to customers" wording → value proposition.

### Value chain and value delivery network

**Value chain**
- What it is: a way of splitting a firm into activities that add value. Two groups: support activities and primary business processes (slide 16). The lecture does not name the author; the textbook version is from Michael Porter.
- Think of it as: a relay race. The runners who carry the baton to the customer are the primary activities. The coach, kit, stadium and selectors are the support activities.
- Spot it in a question: "which activity supports the others" → support. "moves goods or serves the customer" → primary.

**Support activities (four)**
- What it is: Firm infrastructure, Human resource development, Technology development, Procurement (slide 16).
- Think of it as: the stadium, coaches, equipment and selection committee. They help every primary activity but do not touch the product directly.
- Spot it in a question: "firm infrastructure" or "procurement" as the answer to "which is a support activity?" (Mock 2 Q1). If the answer list has an outbound or service option, it is the primary one.

**Primary business processes (five)**
- What it is: Inbound logistics (receiving and storing inputs), Operations (making the product), Outbound logistics (shipping to buyers), Marketing and sales (telling and selling), Service (after-sales help).
- Think of it as: buy, make, ship, sell, serve. The five steps the product passes through.
- Spot it in a question: "shipping to buyers" → outbound logistics (Mock 1 Q2). "after-sales help" → service.

**Value delivery network (also called the supply chain)**
- What it is: the network of suppliers, makers, distributors and customers that moves goods and money along the chain. The slide title equates it with the supply chain.
- Think of it as: the whole relay team across several towns: the fibre mill, the fabric mill, the garment factory and the shop.
- Spot it in a question: "network of partners", "supply chain" wording → value delivery network. "inside one firm's activities" → value chain.

**Procurement, manufacturing, replenishment and customer order cycles**
- What it is: four loops that run along the chain (slide 16). Procurement cycle: buying raw inputs. Manufacturing cycle: turning inputs into goods. Replenishment cycle: restocking the next partner downstream. Customer order cycle: the customer's order running all the way to delivery.
- Think of it as: four loops. In each loop an order goes back upstream and the goods come forward.
- Spot it in a question: "from taking the customer's order through to delivery" → customer order cycle (Mock 2 Q2).

**Business process**
- What it is: a set of linked work steps that run across departments, for example an order passing from sales to shipping to installation to support.
- Think of it as: a dosa order in a restaurant. The waiter, the kitchen and the billing counter must act in sequence.
- Spot it in a question: "redesigned workflows so that teams share information in real time" → business processes (Bank Week 1 Q3).

### Information technology history (1950s to 1990s)

ICT means Information and Communication Technology. The lecture's staircase (slides 19 and 20) shows five eras. Each era adds a new kind of system on top of the last.

**Electronic Data Processing (EDP), 1950 to 1960**
- What it is: computers doing transaction processing, record keeping and traditional accounting.
- Think of it as: a bank passbook machine that records each entry.
- Spot it in a question: "transactions", "record keeping", "traditional accounting" → 1950s.

**Management Information System (MIS), 1960 to 1970**
- What it is: management reports of pre-specified information that support decision making.
- Think of it as: the fixed monthly report with the same columns every month.
- Spot it in a question: "pre-specified", "management reports" → 1960s.

**Decision Support System (DSS), 1970 to 1980**
- What it is: interactive, ad hoc (on-the-spot) support for managerial decision making.
- Think of it as: a what-if spreadsheet where the manager keeps asking new questions and getting answers at once.
- Spot it in a question: "interactive", "ad hoc" → DSS (Mock 2 Q5 answer 1970–1980 decision support).

**Strategic and end-user support systems, 1980 to 1990**
- What it is: end-user computing (managers run their own tools), expert systems (rule-based advisers), and strategic information systems for competitive advantage.
- Think of it as: a manager who builds their own report, or a rule book that answers loan questions automatically.
- Spot it in a question: "competitive advantage", "expert system", "end users" → 1980s.

**E-commerce and e-business systems, 1990 onwards**
- What it is: ERP plus new enabling technologies for supply chain integration and inter-operability. The lecture's list: ERP, web-based systems, web services, cloud computing, mobile computing, RFID, GPS-GIS, Internet of things, business analytics, big data.
- Think of it as: the whole shop wired to its suppliers and customers.
- Spot it in a question: "web", "cloud", "mobile", "RFID", "IoT", "big data", "integration across the chain" → the 1990s onwards era.

**ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning)**
- What it is: one integrated software system with one shared database for finance, human resources, production, sales and purchasing.
- Think of it as: a school with one register for all classes, instead of a separate notebook for each class.
- Spot it in a question: "one integrated system", "departments see the same data" → ERP. Tata Steel began implementing ERP software from SAP (a German software company) in 2000–01.

**RFID (Radio Frequency Identification)**
- What it is: tags on goods that are read by radio, without a line-of-sight scan. Covered properly in Week 8.
- Think of it as: the metro tap-card that is read as you pass the gate.
- Spot it in a question: in Week 1 it only appears in the 1990s list. If a question asks about checking temperature, RFID is the wrong tool (see Week 0 Q5).

**IoT (Internet of Things)**
- What it is: everyday objects with sensors that send data over the network.
- Think of it as: a fridge that tells you the milk is nearly finished.
- Spot it in a question: "sensors", "real-time readings from objects" → IoT.

**GPS-GIS**
- What it is: GPS (Global Positioning System) tells you where a vehicle is. GIS (Geographic Information System) puts that position on a map.
- Think of it as: a delivery van's live dot on a map.
- Spot it in a question: "location of vehicles on a map" → GPS-GIS.

**Business analytics and big data**
- What it is: analysing very large sets of transaction and customer data to find patterns.
- Think of it as: the shopkeeper's notebook of sales, scaled up to millions of records.
- Spot it in a question: "patterns in large data", "customer behaviour from records" → analytics.

**Interoperability**
- What it is: different systems can exchange and understand each other's data.
- Think of it as: a universal charger that works on every phone.
- Spot it in a question: "inter-operability" → the systems can talk to each other.

### Types of e-business transactions

Every transaction type is named by the sender (the first word) and the receiver (the second word). The lecture uses three players: Customer (or consumer), Business, and Government. That makes 3 × 3 = 9 types (slide 25).

**C2C (Consumer to Consumer)**
- What it is: individuals sell to individuals, usually through a platform that charges a fee or shows ads. Lecture example: eBay. Issues: hosting by an intermediary, payment and security.
- Think of it as: a neighbourhood garage sale run through an app. You sell your old bicycle to a stranger, and the app takes a small cut.
- Spot it in a question: "individuals sell to individuals", "platform earns a commission" (Bank Week 1 Q6).

**C2B (Consumer to Business)**
- What it is: an individual offers something to a business, such as a review, a name-your-price bid, or a service. Lecture example: Amazon.com, through customer reviews.
- Think of it as: a customer writing a review that the shop uses to sell more.
- Spot it in a question: "customers review", "customers name the price", "individuals offer services to firms" → C2B (Mock 1 Q8: Amazon.com).

**B2C (Business to Consumer)**
- What it is: a company sells to individual customers online. Lecture example: Dell.com.
- Think of it as: the shop counter, but on a screen.
- Spot it in a question: "a company sells to end users online" (Mock 2 Q8: Dell.com).

**B2B (Business to Business)**
- What it is: companies trade with companies, often automating orders, purchasing and payments. Lecture example: Metaljunction.com, a steel e-market.
- Think of it as: a wholesaler buying from a steel mill.
- Spot it in a question: "both buyer and seller are companies", "steel industry", "automation of business processes" → B2B.

**C2G (Consumer to Government)**
- What it is: citizens pay or apply to government online. Lecture examples: paying taxes and applying for benefits.
- Think of it as: paying your property tax on the municipal portal and downloading the receipt.
- Spot it in a question: "citizen pays property tax", "applies for benefits" → C2G (Bank Week 1 Q5).

**G2C (Government to Citizen)**
- What it is: government serves citizens. Lecture examples: answering public queries, asking citizens for suggestions.
- Think of it as: a municipal help desk that answers questions and runs a public poll on its website.
- Spot it in a question: "government answers public queries", "asks citizens for suggestions" → G2C (Mock 1 Q7). Trap: "paying taxes" is C2G, the reverse direction.

**G2B (Government to Business)**
- What it is: government serves companies. Lecture examples: registration and renewal of licences.
- Think of it as: the trade licence office renewing a shop's licence online.
- Spot it in a question: "registration", "renewal of licences", "a company applies to government" → G2B (Mock 2 Q6).

**B2G (Business to Government)**
- What it is: companies sell products to government buyers. Lecture example: selling steel to government buyers.
- Think of it as: a steel company supplying a state construction agency.
- Spot it in a question: "sells to a government agency", "government buyers" → B2G (Mock 1 Q6: raw steel sold to a state-run construction agency).

**G2G (Government to Government)**
- What it is: government organisations exchange payments electronically with each other. Lecture example: e-payment between government organisations.
- Think of it as: one ministry paying another ministry's bill through a shared system.
- Spot it in a question: "two government organisations", "payments between them" → G2G (Mock 2 Q7).

**Memory trick for the nine types:** the first letter is the sender and the second is the receiver. C2G means the citizen pays the government. G2C means the government serves the citizen.

### Brick-and-mortar plus e-business

**Brick-and-mortar business**
- What it is: a traditional firm with physical shops, offices and factories.
- Think of it as: the old kirana shop with a real building.
- Spot it in a question: "physical stores", "existing offline operations". An online-only firm is the opposite.

**Integrating brick-and-mortar with e-business**
- What it is: linking the old physical operations to web systems so that both channels work as one (slide 37).
- Think of it as: the kirana shop adding an app that is connected to its own stock and billing, not a separate notebook.
- Spot it in a question: "combined physical and online operations", "both channels connected".

**Enterprise system**
- What it is: the core system at the centre of the lecture's diagram. It runs enterprise-wide business processes that connect manufacturing and production, finance and accounting, human resources and sales and marketing.
- Think of it as: the shop's central ledger and noticeboard, which every department writes to.
- Spot it in a question: "business processes that span departments" → enterprise system.

**Organisational boundary**
- What it is: the line between the firm and the outside world. Vendors are on one side and customers on the other.
- Think of it as: the shop door. Stock and supplier orders come in on one side, and customers and their orders go out on the other.
- Spot it in a question: "vendor side" or "customer side" (Mock 2 Q9).

**E-procurement (B2B, vendor side)**
- What it is: buying inputs from vendors electronically. In the lecture's diagram it sits on the vendor side of the boundary.
- Think of it as: ordering stock from the wholesaler's portal instead of by phone and paper.
- Spot it in a question: "buy from vendors online", "suppliers quote electronically", "purchase requisition".

**Supply chain management (SCM), B2B label**
- What it is: planning and moving goods and information from suppliers to customers. The lecture's diagram places it on the customer side, labelled B2B.
- Think of it as: the delivery pipeline from the godown to the shop.
- Spot it in a question: "managing the flow of goods across partners".

**Customer relationship management (CRM), B2C**
- What it is: managing all contacts and data about customers to keep and grow them.
- Think of it as: the shop's register of regulars with their birthdays and last purchases.
- Spot it in a question: "keep and grow customers", "customer data", "loyalty".

**E-marketing, B2C**
- What it is: promoting products through online channels.
- Think of it as: the shop's WhatsApp broadcast and an Instagram ad.
- Spot it in a question: "online promotion", "digital advertising" (sits under sales and marketing in the diagram).

**E-selling and distribution, B2C**
- What it is: selling online and delivering the goods to the buyer.
- Think of it as: the online order arriving with the delivery boy at the door.
- Spot it in a question: "sell online and deliver" (on the customer side of the diagram).

**Organisational website, B2B and B2G**
- What it is: the firm's website also serves investors and government (slide 37 labels: B2B, B2G, investors and government).
- Think of it as: a noticeboard that also serves the bank manager and the tax office.
- Spot it in a question: "investors" or "government" reading the firm's website.

### Business models on the web

**Business model (Teece, 2010)**
- What it is: a business model articulates the logic and gives evidence of how a business creates and delivers value to customers. It also outlines the architecture of revenues, costs and profits (slide 42).
- Think of it as: a recipe card. Ingredients are costs, what the dish sells for is revenue, and what is left on the plate is profit.
- Spot it in a question: "how the firm makes money", "architecture of revenues, costs and profits" → business model (Mock 3 Q1).

**The value-creation cycle (slide 43)**
- What it is: a loop with six steps. Design mechanisms to deliver value; select technologies and features to embed in the product; determine the customer's benefit from using it; identify market segments to target; design mechanisms to capture value; confirm available revenue streams. The loop ends with: create value for customers, entice payments, convert payments to profits.
- Think of it as: a product's design brief and price tag combined in one loop.
- Spot it in a question: "design mechanisms", "revenue streams", "target segments" → business model thinking.

#### Brokerage model

**Brokerage model**
- What it is: brokers are market-makers who bring buyers and sellers together and facilitate the transaction. They usually charge a fee or commission for each transaction. They exist in B2B, B2C and C2C markets (slide 45).
- Think of it as: a property broker or travel agent. They do not own the flat or the ticket; they earn a cut for the match.
- Spot it in a question: "middleman", "brings buyers and sellers together", "fee or commission for each deal". Then choose the variant by the mechanism below.

**Marketplace exchange (Orbitz, ChemConnect)**
- What it is: a market where many buyers and sellers of one industry meet and trade.
- Think of it as: an industry trade fair that runs all year on a screen.
- Spot it in a question: "exchange for one industry".

**Buy/sell fulfilment (CarsDirect, Respond.com)**
- What it is: the broker takes a buyer's request and fulfils it by finding and connecting to sellers.
- Think of it as: a wedding caterer who collects quotes from several caterers for you.
- Spot it in a question: "customer's request is matched to sellers".

**Demand collection system (Priceline.com)**
- What it is: the customer names the price they will pay, and sellers decide whether to accept it.
- Think of it as: a "name your own price" counter. "I will pay Rs 3,000 for this seat."
- Spot it in a question: "customer names the price", "sellers decide whether to accept" (Mock 3 Q5: an airline seat).

**Auction broker (eBay)**
- What it is: sellers list items, buyers bid, and the broker earns a commission on each completed sale.
- Think of it as: a local auction house that earns a commission on the hammer price.
- Spot it in a question: "bids", "auction", "commission on each completed sale" (Mock 2 Q10).

**Transaction broker (PayPal, Escrow.com)**
- What it is: a third party handles the payment between buyer and seller and charges a fee. It often holds the money until the goods arrive.
- Think of it as: a trusted uncle who keeps the money in his drawer until both sides have done their part.
- Spot it in a question: "holds payment until goods arrive", "releases the money to the seller" (Mock 3 Q7). PayPal's model: the charges are taken out of the payment to the seller (slide 47).

**Distributor, catalogue services**
- What it is: a broker that distributes a catalogue of products from many suppliers.
- Think of it as: a wholesale catalogue or a directory of suppliers you can order from.
- Spot it in a question: "catalogue of products from many suppliers".

**Search agent (price bots, shop bots)**
- What it is: software that searches many shops and reports the cheapest price or best option.
- Think of it as: a shopping friend who visits twenty shops for you and reports back.
- Spot it in a question: "automatically checks many online stores and shows the lowest price" (Mock 3 Q6).

**Virtual marketplace (Amazon.com, as a broker)**
- What it is: an online market where many sellers and buyers meet on one platform that runs the market.
- Think of it as: an online mall, with stalls run by many shopkeepers.
- Spot it in a question: "many sellers list on one platform". Trap: Amazon also appears as a virtual merchant, which means it sells its own stock (see the merchant model).

#### Advertising model

**Advertising model**
- What it is: the web version of the TV or radio broadcast model. The site provides content and services mixed with advertising messages, usually banner ads. Ads may be the main or only income. The site may create its own content or distribute content made elsewhere. It works best when viewer traffic is large or highly specialised (slide 48).
- Think of it as: a cricket broadcast with ad breaks. The viewers are the audience that advertisers pay to reach.
- Spot it in a question: "free content", "banner ads", "advertisers pay the site" (Mock 1 Q14). Trap: "subscription" is the users paying the site, not advertisers.

**Portal (Yahoo!)**
- What it is: a big gateway site with news, mail, search and other services, carrying ads.
- Think of it as: a mall entrance covered with billboards.
- Spot it in a question: "gateway site", "news and search with advertising".

**Classifieds (Monster.com, Craigslist, Match.com)**
- What it is: online listings for jobs, rentals, products or matches, earning from ads or listing fees.
- Think of it as: the classified pages of a newspaper, moved online.
- Spot it in a question: "job listings and rental adverts posted by users" → classifieds (Mock 3 Q8).

**Query-based paid placement (Google)**
- What it is: advertisers bid to appear against specific search queries, and are charged for each placement that a query triggers (slide 48, slide 51).
- Think of it as: a shop paying to have its name shown first when someone searches for "kashmiri shawl".
- Spot it in a question: "bid for placement against search queries" → paid placement (Mock 2 Q14).

**Banner ads**
- What it is: rectangular ads placed on a web page.
- Think of it as: the hoarding at a cricket ground.
- Spot it in a question: "banner" in the question or the options.

#### Infomediary model

**Infomediary (information intermediary)**
- What it is: a firm that helps buyers and/or sellers understand a market. Its functions are target marketing campaigns, comparative purchasing, advertising networks and audience measurement (slide 53).
- Think of it as: a TV-rating agency or a market research firm that sells the numbers to brands.
- Spot it in a question: "measures audience size and viewing behaviour and sells it to advertisers" (Mock 2 Q12). Trap: an advertising site earns from ads on its own content; an infomediary sells information about audiences or markets.

**Advertising network (DoubleClick)**

### Hype and bubble

**Gartner Hype Cycle**
- What it is: a graph developed and used by the Gartner group. It represents the maturity, adoption and social application of specific technologies over time (slide 40). Five stages in order: technology trigger, peak of inflated expectations, trough of disillusionment, slope of enlightenment, plateau of productivity.
- Think of it as: a roller coaster. A fast climb, a fall, a slow climb back up, and then a flat level.
- Spot it in a question: "which stage" questions. Read the stem: excitement and funding → peak; collapse and failures → trough; real use cases → slope; mainstream and stable → plateau.

**Technology trigger (called Innovation Trigger in the question bank)**
- What it is: the first stage. A new technology appears and gets early attention. The lecture slide says "Technology Trigger"; the question bank's option says "Innovation Trigger". They are the same first stage.
- Think of it as: the first product launch and the first buzz.
- Spot it in a question: either name → the first stage.

**Peak of inflated expectations**
- What it is: the hype is at its height. Money flows in and expectations run ahead of reality.
- Think of it as: the dot-com frenzy, where any internet startup got funding.
- Spot it in a question: "technological excitement", "investors fund many internet startups despite weak business fundamentals". The collapse comes after this stage (Bank Week 1 Q7).

**Trough of disillusionment**
- What it is: the crash. Expectations fall and many firms fail.
- Think of it as: the morning after the party.
- Spot it in a question: "many of these firms collapse a few years later" → trough (Bank Week 1 Q7, answer D).

**Slope of enlightenment**
- What it is: practical uses are found and the survivors learn how to make money.
- Think of it as: the second year, when survivors work out what customers really pay for.
- Spot it in a question: "practical use cases", "learning how to benefit".

**Plateau of productivity**
- What it is: the technology is mainstream, stable and widely used.
- Think of it as: email today. Everyone uses it, nobody talks about it.
- Spot it in a question: "mainstream", "stable", "widely adopted".

**Dot-com boom and bust (chart, 1994 to 2002)**
- What it is: a chart of the NASDAQ (a US stock market) total market value, adapted from Kampas Research and Eric Janszen's work. Its stage labels: Normal → Formation → Hyperinflation → Dissipation → Overshoot → Normal. Its labels mark: money from venture capital at the start; the emergence of the World Wide Web; the founding of Amazon.com; the founding of Google; a sales tax moratorium on e-commerce passed by Congress. The value peaked at about $3.7 trillion, with about $2.6 trillion of it fictitious (above the historical trend), then fell to about $1.1 trillion on the chart. The chart's footnote: "6 years up, 2 years down".
- Think of it as: a balloon blown up very fast and then let down.
- Spot it in a question: "dot-com", "internet bubble", "internet stocks crash".

**Problems of the mandi system (slides 90, 91)**
- What it is: nine problems, grouped here:

## How it flows

### 1. The business purpose loop
```text
  Deliver value ──► Satisfy the customer ──► Customer stays loyal ──► Steady revenue ──► Profit
  (benefits beat cost)  (performance meets expectation)  (repeat buying)
```
Each step depends on the one before. No value means no satisfaction; no loyalty means no steady revenue.

### 2. Customer delivered value (Kotler)
```text
  Total customer value   −   Total customer cost   =   Customer delivered value
  (bundle of benefits)       (money, time, effort,      (net gain the customer
                              hassle, disposal)           feels)
```
Cut the cost (faster process, simpler returns) or add benefits, and the delivered value rises.

### 3. Satisfaction is a comparison
```text
  Expectation ─────────┐
                       ├──► compare ──► Better than expected ──► Pleasure ──► Loyal customer
  Perceived performance┘               Worse than expected  ──► Disappointment ──► Customer leaves
```
The word "satisfaction" always means this comparison. The word "value" means the arithmetic above.

### 4. Value chain (inside one firm, slide 16)
```text
  SUPPORT ACTIVITIES (run across the top, help everything below)
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Firm infrastructure                                          │
  │  Human resource development                                   │
  │  Technology development                                       │
  │  Procurement                                                  │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  PRIMARY BUSINESS PROCESSES (run left to right)
  Inbound ──► Operations ──► Outbound ──► Marketing ──► Service
  logistics                   logistics     and sales
```
Support activities are the stadium and coaches. Primary activities are the runners who move the product to the customer.

### 5. Value delivery network (the supply chain, slide 16)
```text
  Supplier's supplier ──► Supplier ──► The firm ──► Distributor ──► Customer
   DuPont (fibres)       Milliken      Levi's       Sears (retail)   Customer
                         (fabric)      (apparel)

  ◄────────── ORDER travels back up the chain ───────────────────
  ──────────► DELIVERY travels down the chain ──────────────────►
```
Lecture reading: DuPont is the supplier's supplier, Milliken the supplier, Levi's the firm itself, Sears the distributor.

### 6. The four cycles along the chain (slide 16, reading the arcs under the chain)
```text
  DuPont ──── Procurement cycle ──── Milliken ──── Manufacturing cycle ──── Levi's
  Levi's ──── Replenishment cycle ──── Sears ──── Customer order cycle ──── Customer
```
Procurement: buy inputs. Manufacturing: make goods. Replenishment: restock downstream. Customer order: from the customer's order to delivery.

### 7. ICT history staircase (slides 19 and 20)
```text
  1990s on  ──  E-commerce and e-business systems   (ERP + web, cloud, RFID, IoT, analytics)
```

## Don't mix these up

| These look alike | How to tell them apart | Tie-breaker word |
|---|---|---|
| Business vs commerce | Commerce is buying and selling. Business is wider and includes making goods and managing people. | "subset" (commerce is the smaller circle) |
| E-commerce vs e-business | E-commerce is buying, selling, marketing and servicing goods online. E-business covers every business process, including internal work and partners. | "superset" (e-business is bigger) |
| Total customer value vs total customer cost vs customer delivered value | Value = bundle of benefits. Cost = bundle of costs. Delivered value = value minus cost. | "minus" → delivered value |
| Customer satisfaction vs customer delivered value | Satisfaction is a feeling from comparing performance with expectation. Delivered value is a net amount. | "feeling" → satisfaction |
| Value proposition vs value-delivery system | Proposition is the promise to the customer. Delivery system is how the promise is kept. | "promise" vs "how" |
| Primary vs support activities | Primary (buy, make, ship, sell, serve) touch the product flow. Support (firm infrastructure, HR development, technology development, procurement) help all of them. | "customer sees it" → primary |
| Value chain vs value delivery network | Value chain = activities inside one firm. Value delivery network = the wider chain of partners, the supply chain. | "partners" → network |
| Procurement vs replenishment vs customer order cycle | Procurement = buying inputs. Replenishment = restocking downstream. Customer order = order to delivery. | "from the customer's order" → customer order cycle |
| Business process vs business function | A business process runs across departments (order to delivery). A function is one department's job. | "across departments" |
| EDP vs MIS | EDP records transactions. MIS produces pre-set management reports. | "transactions" vs "reports" |
| MIS vs DSS | MIS = pre-specified reports. DSS = interactive, ad hoc decision support. | "pre-specified" vs "ad hoc" |
| DSS (1970s) vs strategic and end-user systems (1980s) | 1980s adds competitive advantage, expert systems and managers running their own tools. | "competitive advantage" → 1980s |
| ERP vs e-business | ERP is one integrated internal system. E-business is the wider online operation including partners and web. | "one system" → ERP |
| B2B vs B2C | B2B: company to company (Metaljunction). B2C: company to individual customer (Dell.com). | "who is the buyer, a company or a person?" |
| C2C vs C2B | C2C: person to person (eBay). C2B: person offers something to a company (Amazon reviews). | "who is the receiver?" |
| B2C (transaction type) vs manufacturer (direct) model | B2C is who trades with whom. Manufacturer (direct) is how the company earns, by cutting out middlemen. Dell appears in both. | "type" (who) vs "model" (how it earns) |
| C2G vs G2C | C2G: citizen pays or applies to government (tax, benefits). G2C: government serves citizens (queries, suggestions). | first letter is the sender |
| G2B vs B2G | G2B: government serves a company (licence renewal). B2G: company sells to government. | "who is selling?" |
| G2G vs G2C | G2G: between two government bodies. G2C: government to citizen. | both sides government? |
| Brick-and-mortar vs click-and-mortar | Brick-and-mortar is physical only. Click-and-mortar is physical plus web (Barnes & Noble). | "click" = web added |
| Vendor side vs customer side | Vendor side: e-procurement (buying). Customer side: CRM, e-marketing, e-selling and distribution, and supply chain management (selling). | "buying" vs "selling" |
| E-marketing vs e-selling and distribution | E-marketing promotes online. E-selling and distribution sells and delivers. | "promote" vs "deliver" |
| Brokerage vs merchant | A broker never owns the goods and earns a fee. A merchant buys and owns the goods and earns a margin. | "owns the goods?" |
| Auction broker (eBay) vs reverse auction (Tata) | eBay: sellers sell, bids go up, broker takes commission. Reverse auction: buyer buys, suppliers bid the price down. | "who is buying?" |
| Auction broker vs transaction broker | Auction broker runs the bidding and takes a commission. Transaction broker handles the money, often holding it until delivery. | "bids" vs "holds money" |
| Demand collection (Priceline) vs auction broker | Demand collection: customer names the price, sellers accept or reject. Auction: bidders compete. | "name your price" |
| Search agent vs virtual marketplace | Search agent compares prices across many shops. Virtual marketplace hosts many sellers on one platform. | "compare" vs "host" |
| Virtual merchant vs virtual marketplace (Amazon) | Virtual merchant owns the stock it sells. Virtual marketplace only hosts other sellers. | "owns the stock?" |
| Catalog merchant vs virtual merchant | Catalog merchant sells from a printed or online catalogue (Lands' End). Virtual merchant is online only (Amazon as seller). | "catalogue" |
| Bit vendor vs virtual merchant | Bit vendor sells digital downloads (iTunes). Virtual merchant sells goods online, possibly physical. | "download" |
| Advertising vs infomediary | Advertising earns from ads on its own content (Yahoo!). Infomediary sells information about markets and audiences (Nielsen, DoubleClick). | "information for sale" |
| Portal vs classifieds vs query-based paid placement | Portal = gateway with mixed services (Yahoo!). Classifieds = listings (Monster). Paid placement = bids on search words (Google). | "search keywords bid" → Google |
| Banner exchange (affiliate) vs banner ads (advertising) | Banner exchange: two sites swap banners. Banner ads: advertisers pay for ads on a content site. | "swap" vs "advertisers pay" |
| Affiliate pay-per-click vs paid placement | Affiliate pay-per-click pays a partner for sending a visitor to a merchant. Paid placement is an ad shown on search results. | "partner's link" |
| Affiliate vs brokerage | Affiliate: merchant pays commission for referred sales. Brokerage: broker brings two parties together and charges a fee per deal. | "merchant" vs "two parties" |
| Community vs subscription | Community: voluntary donations, services, contextual ads. Subscription: fixed periodic fee. | "voluntary" vs "fee" |
| Trust services vs transaction broker | Trust services: members pay for a membership seal and follow a code of conduct. Transaction broker: handles the money between buyer and seller. | "code of conduct" vs "money" |
| Subscription vs metered subscription vs utility | Subscription = fixed periodic fee. Metered subscription = prepaid portions (e.g. pages). Utility = pay per actual use. | "fixed" / "portions" / "usage" |
| ISP as subscription vs ISP as utility | Same kind of provider, two models. Monthly plan (AOL) = subscription. Per connection minute = utility. | "monthly" vs "per minute" |
| Metered usage vs metered subscription | Metered usage bills actual usage (minutes, gigabytes). Metered subscription sells portions (pages viewed). | "actual usage" vs "portions" |
| Technology trigger vs innovation trigger | Same first stage of the hype cycle, two names. | none needed |
| Peak vs trough | Peak = hype and money in. Trough = crash and failures. | "collapse" → trough |
| Slope vs plateau | Slope = survivors learn and climb. Plateau = flat, stable, mainstream. | "learning" vs "stable" |
| Hype cycle vs dot-com chart | Hype cycle is a general graph for any technology (Gartner). Dot-com chart is one market bubble (NASDAQ, 1994–2002). | "NASDAQ" → dot-com chart |
| Reverse auction vs e-negotiation vs Metaljunction | Reverse auction: suppliers bid price down (high volume, not critical). E-negotiation: bargaining over terms (high volume, critical). Metaljunction: scan the whole market (low volume, critical). | "bid down" / "bargain" / "scan" |
| Partnership with suppliers vs business process outsourcing | Partnership: long-term tie-up on critical, high-volume items. BPO: outsource routine, low-volume, not-critical work. | "critical" → partnership |
| Mandi (traditional) vs eChoupal (new) | Mandi: agents, bagging, auctions, commission. eChoupal: kiosk, web prices, electronic weighing, hub. | "agent" vs "kiosk" |
| Prathinidhi vs adatiya | Prathinidhi: local farmer who runs the kiosk for the village. Adatiya: commission agent who lends money and takes a cut. | "helps the village" vs "takes a cut" |
| Kuccha vs pucca adatiya | Kuccha: farm-end, linking farmers to the market yard. Pucca: market-end, linking to exporters and processors. | "temporary" vs "permanent" (kuccha = unpaved, pucca = permanent) |
| Tata Steel vs ITC eChoupal | Tata: steel company buying with SAP and online systems. eChoupal: ITC buying soybeans from farmers through village kiosks. | "steel and SAP" → Tata; "village and soy" → eChoupal |
| Business vs business model | A business is the firm. A business model is how it creates and captures value (revenues, costs, profits). | "how it makes money" → business model |

## How this week gets asked

**Question patterns you will see**
- "Scenario → name the model or transaction type": a short story (a firm, a seller, a government office) and the answer is a code (C2G) or a model name (affiliate). Most common.
- "Who coined it or said it": Lou Gerstner (e-business), Andy Grove (1999 quote), Kotler (customer delivered value), Aaker (business definition).
- "Which is NOT ..." or "which is NOT a benefit of ...": the answer is the one that does not appear on the slide list (Mock 3 Q3: physically stocking goods in a warehouse).
- "Which stage / cycle / activity": hype-cycle stage from a story, the cycle from a delivery description, the primary or support activity.
- "Read the chart": the steepest fall, the advantage named, the price gain (Mock 3 Q14, Mock 3 Q15).
- "Case detail": which Tata year, which portfolio box, which eChoupal kiosk person or number (Mock 2 Q13, Mock 2 Q15, Mock 1 Q9).
- "Vendor side or customer side": which activity sits where in the boundary diagram (Mock 2 Q9).

**Distractor traps for this week**
- True statement, wrong question: a correct fact about another model or another side of the boundary (for example "Dell.com" when the question asks about C2B).
- Reversed direction: G2C vs C2G, "business is a subset of commerce" vs "commerce is a subset of business", "e-commerce is a superset of e-business".
- Absolute words: "only", "eliminates", "always", "never". Week 1 Mock 1 Q5 uses "covers only buying and selling" as a wrong option; Week 0 Q8 uses "eliminates" as a wrong option.
- Concepts from other weeks: RFID, GPS, IoT and EDI (Electronic Data Interchange, Weeks 7–8), SCM (supply chain management, Week 4), auctions (Week 11), recommender systems (Week 12). If the question is about the Week 1 lecture, pick the Week 1 term.
- One company in several roles: Amazon is a C2B example, a virtual merchant, a virtual marketplace and an affiliate partner. Barnes & Noble is a click-and-mortar merchant and an affiliate partner. eBay is a C2C example and an auction broker. Dell is a B2C example and a manufacturer (direct) model. Read the question's mechanism, not the company name.
- Subscription wording on a metered product: "metered subscription" is a utility model.
- Similar names for the same stage: "technology trigger" and "innovation trigger" are the same stage.
- Hype and bubble words: "peak" (the excitement) vs "trough" (the crash).
- Near-miss numbers: "2.5% higher" is the farmers' price gain; "$6 per tonne" is the same gain in dollars. A wrong option may swap in a different percentage.

**Rules to use in the exam**
1. If the question says "a citizen pays or applies to a government office" → C2G.
2. If the question says "government answers citizens or asks them for suggestions" → G2C.
3. If the question says "companies buy from companies, scanning the market or automated orders" → B2B (Metaljunction).
4. If the question says "a fee or commission for bringing two parties together" → brokerage. Then choose the variant by the mechanism: bids → auction broker; holds money → transaction broker; customer names the price → demand collection; compares many shops → search agent.
5. If the question says "free content, banner ads, advertisers pay" → advertising model. If it says "measures audience and sells data" → infomediary.
6. If it says "pay per use, metered" → utility. If it says "fixed periodic fee" → subscription. If it says "voluntary donations or community content" → community.
7. If it says "sells directly to customers, cutting out distributors" → manufacturer (direct).
8. If it says "commission when a visitor buys through a link" → affiliate.
9. If it says "excitement and heavy funding, then many firms fail" → peak then trough. If it asks about the crash itself → trough.
10. If it says "a single integrated system used by all departments" → ERP. If it says "reports on fixed items" → MIS. If it says "interactive, ad hoc decisions" → DSS.

## Practice walk-through

**Q1.** A founder has a popular app, no clear revenue model, and no one owns profit. Which strategic element is missing?
- Answer: A) A defined strategy and profit responsibility
- Why: Aaker's definition of a business needs a defined strategy and a manager with sales and profit responsibility (Business and commerce cards).
- Trap: D) A large customer base. Many users do not give a business a plan or an owner of profit.

**Q2.** A software company adds free onboarding, faster support and a simpler return process. What is most likely to increase?
- Answer: C) Customer delivered value
- Why: Customer delivered value is total customer value minus total customer cost (Customer value cards). Faster support and simpler returns add benefits and cut hassle.
- Trap: A) Total customer cost. Those changes reduce the cost the customer bears, so cost goes down, not up.

**Q3.** A company redesigns workflows so procurement, production and customer service share information in real time. What does it mainly improve?
- Answer: D) Business processes
- Why: A business process is a workflow linking departments (high-performance business card, slide 17).
- Trap: B) Resource ownership. Resources are labour, materials, machines, information and energy, not the workflow itself.

**Q4.** Which statement best distinguishes commerce from business?
- Answer: D) Commerce primarily concerns buying and selling activities.
- Why: Commerce is the buying and selling activity (Oxford definition). It is a subset of business (Commerce card).
- Trap: C) Business is a subset of commerce. That is the reverse of the lecture.

**Q5.** A citizen pays property tax through an online government portal and downloads the receipt. Which transaction type is this?
- Answer: C) C2G
- Why: The citizen is the sender and the government is the receiver (transaction types card, grid).
- Trap: D) G2C. That is the government serving the citizen, the other direction.

**Q6.** An online marketplace lets individuals sell used bicycles to other individuals and earns a small commission. Which transaction type is this?
- Answer: B) C2C
- Why: Both sides are individuals, and the platform only facilitates and earns a commission (C2C card; brokerage card).
- Trap: C) B2C. A business would have to be one of the parties.

**Q7.** Investors fund many internet startups despite weak fundamentals, and a few years later many collapse. Which stage of the Gartner Hype Cycle matches the collapse?
- Answer: D) Trough of Disillusionment
- Why: The collapse after the hype is the trough (Hype and bubble cards; slide 40).
- Trap: C) Peak of Inflated Expectations. The funding frenzy is the peak; the collapse comes after it.

**Q8.** A website offers free tech news and forums, earns from firms that pay to reach visitors, and users create and curate content. Which combination of models fits?
- Answer: C) Advertising Model + Community Model
- Why: Firms paying to reach visitors is the advertising model (banner ads). Voluntary user-created content is the community model (business models cards).
- Trap: A) Subscription Model + Utility Model. The site is free, with no periodic fee and no metered usage.

**Q9.** A company wants to stop distributors blocking its view of customer needs, so it sells online with configuration, direct orders and support. Which model supports this?
- Answer: B) Manufacturer (Direct) Model
- Why: Reaching buyers directly and understanding customer preferences is the manufacturer (direct) model, as with Dell (Manufacturer card).
- Trap: A) Brokerage Model. A broker adds a middleman; the company here wants to remove one.

**Q10.** A blogger places links to an online bookstore and earns a commission on each purchase through those links. Which model is this?
- Answer: A) Affiliate Model
- Why: Affiliates provide purchase-point click-through and are paid for results (affiliate model card).
- Trap: D) Infomediary Model. An infomediary sells information about markets and audiences, not commissions on sales.

## Practice walk-through: Week 0 (course overview)

Week 0 has no lecture file. The "Why" lines point to the course map (slides 2 to 7 of the Week 1 file: Week 1 introduction, Weeks 2 to 4 functional areas, Weeks 5 to 8 technologies, Weeks 9 to 12 decision support) and to the Week 1 concept cards that are closest.

**Q1.** An online retailer sees umbrella sales spike when heavy rain is forecast and wants the website to respond automatically. Which capability helps most?
- Answer: A) Continuously adapting prices and promotions using business data
- Why: Automatic, data-driven decisions are decision support (ICT history: DSS and business analytics; course map Weeks 9 to 12).
- Trap: B) Periodically redesigning the homepage. That is periodic and manual, not data-driven or automatic.

**Q2.** Procurement, inventory, finance and sales report different values for the same stock. Which improvement most directly fixes this?
- Answer: B) Integrating business functions through a common information system
- Why: One shared system across departments is ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) (ICT history card, ERP).
- Trap: C) Increasing the frequency of inventory audits every quarter. Audits check the numbers after the fact and do not create one shared record.

**Q3.** A procurement portal automatically checks delivery schedules, contract conditions and budget limits before selecting a supplier. Which feature distinguishes this from ordinary e-mail?
- Answer: A) Business decisions are supported using predefined organizational rules
- Why: Rule-based decision support inside an automated information system (E-business card; course map Weeks 3 to 4 on e-procurement, and decision support).
- Trap: B) Supplier quotations are transmitted through secure Internet protocols. That is about transport security, which e-mail can also have.

**Q4.** Shoppers buying breakfast cereal also buy fresh milk. Which question does this observation relate to most closely?
- Answer: A) How can purchasing patterns be discovered from transaction records?
- Why: Finding buying patterns in transaction data is web analytics and customer behaviour modelling (course map: Weeks 9 and 10).
- Trap: B) How can inventory be replenished at fixed time intervals? That is a restocking schedule, not a pattern search.

**Q5.** An exporter wants overseas buyers to verify that fruit stayed within temperature range, without relying on paper. Which combination fits best?
- Answer: D) IoT sensing with trusted digital traceability records
- Why: Sensors that report live readings are IoT (ICT history card, IoT; course map Week 8).
- Trap: B) RFID tracking with automated warehouse scheduling. RFID tells you where a tagged item is, not its temperature.

**Q6.** A shopping site shows different products to different customers searching the same keyword. Which question naturally arises?
- Answer: B) How are search and recommendation results determined?
- Why: Search and recommendation ranking is a Week 12 topic (course map: recommender systems).
- Trap: A) How should products be packed before shipment? That is about packaging, not ranking.

**Q7.** A government wants to allocate spectrum licences through an online auction, and the highest bid alone may not give the best allocation. What makes this hard?
- Answer: C) Multiple business constraints must be satisfied simultaneously
- Why: Auction design with several constraints is the Week 11 auctions topic (course map: decision support, auctions).
- Trap: B) Bidders may revise offers before the auction closes. That is a rule detail, not the source of the difficulty.

**Q8.** A logistics company records vehicle location, fuel use, delivery time and refrigeration temperature every few minutes. Why is this valuable?
- Answer: A) It enables operational decisions using real-time business information
- Why: Continuous data supports decisions as they happen (ICT history: business analytics and decision support).
- Trap: D) It eliminates the need for warehouse inventory management. "Eliminates" is an absolute claim; data helps but does not remove the need.

**Q9.** Some sellers get more attention even though their prices are not the lowest. Which analytical question is most relevant?
- Answer: A) Which customer and seller characteristics influence purchasing decisions?
- Why: Customer behaviour modelling (course map: Week 10).
- Trap: D) Which suppliers provide the shortest manufacturing lead times? That is about supply speed, not buyer attention.

**Q10.** A checkout delay of one second makes many customers abandon their purchases. Which broad challenge is the company addressing?
- Answer: A) Maintaining fast, secure, and reliable digital business operations
- Why: Speed, security and reliability at scale are e-business operations challenges (course overview: performance and capacity planning, slide 23).
- Trap: B) Reducing the variety of products offered through the platform. Variety does not cause checkout delays.

## 60-second recap

- Business = an organisation that creates value and earns revenue. Commerce = buying and selling, a subset of business.
- E-commerce = buying and selling online. E-business = every business process on automated systems, including partners and internal work.
- Customer delivered value = total customer value minus total customer cost (Kotler).
- Satisfaction = performance compared with expectation. Loyalty comes from high customer value.
- Value chain: four support activities plus five primary activities (buy, make, ship, sell, serve).
- Four cycles: procurement, manufacturing, replenishment, customer order. The customer order cycle runs from order to delivery.
- ICT eras: 50s data processing, 60s MIS, 70s DSS, 80s strategic and end-user, 90s onwards e-business and ERP.
- Nine transaction types: C2C, C2B, B2C, B2B, C2G, G2C, G2B, B2G, G2G. Sender first, receiver second.
- Nine business models: brokerage, advertising, infomediary, merchant, manufacturer (direct), affiliate, community, subscription, utility.
- Broker = fee per deal (auction broker, transaction broker, demand collection, search agent). Merchant = owns the goods.
- Gartner hype cycle: trigger, peak, trough, slope, plateau. The dot-com boom and bust is the same shape for one market.
- Tata Steel: e-procurement timeline; reverse auction; partnership and e-negotiation; BPO; Metaljunction.com for low-volume, critical items when you must scan the market.
- eChoupal: village kiosks run by a Prathinidhi, a portal with seven information areas, direct buying, lower farmer costs per tonne, 2.5% higher price for farmers.
- Mock questions are unseen practice: when a mock asks for a chart value or a portfolio box, read the figure from the chart in "Must-remember facts", not from memory.
