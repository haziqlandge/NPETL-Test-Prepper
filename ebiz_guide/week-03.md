# Week 3 — Stakeholder Portals, E-Procurement and CRM

## In one breath

This week has three linked stories. First, a company talks to its stakeholders through two faces: a public corporate website, and an enterprise portal that gives staff one personalised gateway to internal and outside information. Second, the procurement process (deciding what to buy, finding suppliers, getting prices, buying, and checking supplier performance) is reengineered with information and communication technology (ICT), mostly through online auctions, catalogues and request documents (RFx). Third, customer relationship management (CRM) uses the same data to win, grow and keep profitable customers. Web analytics runs through all three, measuring what visitors do.

**The big analogy:** a big wedding. The corporate website is the printed invitation that anyone can read. The enterprise portal is the family WhatsApp group and shared album that only the family can open. Procurement is the caterer hunt: write the menu, ask several caterers for quotes, pick the best value, then check how the caterer performed. CRM is the family's guest list that remembers who came last time, what they liked, and who should get the next invitation.

## Concept cards

### Corporate website vs enterprise portal

**Corporate website**
- What it is: an informational website that acts as the company's interface to the public (slide 4). Typical contents on slide 9: homepage, "about us" (summary, history, mission), product and service list, "people" (founders, board, executives), news (press releases, press kits), investor section (annual report, financial statements, stock price, SEC (US Securities and Exchange Commission) filings), key clients, pages of special interest groups, employment section, contact information, feedback form, terms of use and privacy policy.
- Think of it as: the shop window and the printed brochure. Anyone walking past can read it.
- Spot it in a question: "interface to the public", "informational", "investor section", "press releases" → corporate website. Bank Week 3 Q1 contrasts it with the portal.

**Corporate website features (slides 10 and 11)**
- What it is: navigation bar; unified look and feel with logos and style sheets; responsive design for screens and browsers; fast response time; improved navigation; personalisation. Extras: splash page (entry point that directs users to the home page), embedded search, site map, blog, community pages (environment, charity, citizenship), store locator, downloads and media, calendar or events, links page, FAQ (frequently asked questions).
- Think of it as: the shop's layout. Signs, a map of the floors, and a helper at the door.
- Spot it in a question: "entry point that directs users to the home page" → splash page (Mock 3 Q1). "Biographical information on founders and executives" → the people section (Mock 2 Q1).

**Enterprise information portal**
- What it is: a web-based interface and integration of MIS (management information systems), DSS (decision support systems), EIS (executive information systems) and other technologies. It gives intranet users, and selected extranet users, access to internal and external business applications and services (slide 4).
- Think of it as: the family WhatsApp group and shared album. Only the family can enter, and each member sees what they need.
- Spot it in a question: "single personalised interface", "employees", "internal and external applications" → enterprise portal (Bank Week 3 Q1).

**Enterprise portal: internal applications (slide 5)**
- What it is: access to e-mail; project websites; discussion groups; human resources (HR) web self-services; customer, inventory and other corporate databases; decision support systems; knowledge management systems.
- Think of it as: the office's internal drawers, all in one room.
- Spot it in a question: "project websites", "HR self-service", "corporate databases" → internal application (Mock 1 Q2).

**Enterprise portal: external applications (slide 6)**
- What it is: industry, financial and other internet news services; links to industry discussion groups; links to customer and supplier internet and extranet websites.
- Think of it as: the newspaper stand and the phone book at the office entrance.
- Spot it in a question: "industry news", "links to supplier extranet sites".

**Enterprise portal: personalisation and benefits (slides 7 and 8)**
- What it is: portals are tailored to individual users or groups, giving each a personalised digital dashboard of information sources and applications. Benefits: more specific and selective information; easy access to key intranet resources; direct access to industry and business news; access to company data for selected customers, suppliers or partners; less excessive surfing by employees.
- Think of it as: a personal home screen that shows only what your job needs.
- Spot it in a question: "employees spend less time searching across unrelated websites" → easier access through a centralised gateway (Bank Week 3 Q2).

### Web analytics

**Web analytics**
- What it is: the measurement, collection, analysis and reporting of web usage data to understand and optimise web design. It covers navigation, content and users (slide 12).
- Think of it as: a shop counting how many people walk past the window, which aisle they visit and where they leave.
- Spot it in a question: "measures, collects, analyses and reports web usage" → web analytics (Mock 2 Q4).

**Web metrics (slide 13)**
- What it is: hits (every file request); page views (pages seen); visits or sessions; click paths (the route through pages); traffic sources (where visitors came from: search engines, social media, referral sites).
- Think of it as: footfall counts, walk-in routes, and where customers came from.
- Spot it in a question: "arrive from search engines, social media or referral sites" → traffic sources (Bank Week 3 Q3). Mock 3 Q3 asks for one of the metrics → hits.

**Web analytics tools (slide 14)**
- What it is: clickstream analysis; Google Analytics, which uses page tagging (a small script on each page that records visits).
- Think of it as: a sticker on each shelf that records each customer who passes.
- Spot it in a question: "page tagging" → Google Analytics technique (Mock 2 Q5).

### Procurement, purchasing and sourcing

**Procurement (David N. Burt, 1984)**
- What it is: the systematic process of deciding what, when and how much to purchase; the act of purchasing it; and ensuring that what is required is received on time in the specified quantity and quality (slide 18).
- Think of it as: the whole chain from the shopping list to the goods arriving in good condition.
- Spot it in a question: "verifying that ordered materials are delivered on schedule and meet agreed specifications" → procurement, the part beyond purchasing and sourcing (Bank Week 3 Q4).

**Purchasing (L. Lee, 1971)**
- What it is: the act of buying materials and services of the right quality, right quantity, right price, from the right source, and at the right time (slide 19).
- Think of it as: the buying trip to the market: the right goods, amount, price, shop and time.
- Spot it in a question: "right quality, right quantity, right price, right source, right time" → purchasing (Mock 2 Q2).

**Sourcing (L. Lee, 1971)**
- What it is: the act of finding, evaluating and engaging suppliers of goods and services (slide 19).
- Think of it as: scouting for the best caterer, comparing their menus and then hiring one.
- Spot it in a question: "finding, evaluating and engaging suppliers" → sourcing (Mock 1 Q3).

**Memory trick:** Sourcing finds the supplier. Purchasing buys. Procurement does both and then makes sure the goods arrive right.

### E-procurement

**E-procurement (online procurement)**
- What it is: a technology solution to facilitate corporate buying using the internet and other ICT (slide 20).
- Think of it as: ordering stock through a wholesaler's app rather than a phone call and paper quotes.
- Spot it in a question: "technology solution to facilitate corporate buying" → e-procurement (Mock 3 Q2).

**Benefits of e-procurement (slide 21)**
- What it is: reduced administrative costs; shortened order fulfilment cycle; lowered inventory levels; lowered price paid for goods; increased technological collaboration and planning with business partners.
- Think of it as: less paperwork, faster delivery, less stock sitting idle, better prices, and better teamwork with suppliers.
- Spot it in a question: "shortened order fulfilment cycle" is a benefit (Mock 1 Q4). "Higher inventory" or "reduced collaboration" are the opposite of benefits.

### Traditional and reengineered procurement

**Traditional purchasing process (adapted from A.J. van Weele, 1994; slide 26)**
- What it is: four stages:
  - Define requirement: specification development, early purchasing and supplier involvement (EPI/ESI, the lecture does not expand these), cross-functional teams.
  - Select supplier: pre-qualification, request for proposal (RFP), select supplier.
  - Contract agreement: negotiate, formalise the contract, establish ordering routines and transaction processing.
  - Supplier evaluation: assess supplier performance, supplier rating.
- Think of it as: a manual approval chain, with paper and meetings at each step.
- Spot it in a question: "rating a supplier's performance" → supplier evaluation (Mock 2 Q6).

**Reengineered purchasing (Guttman, Kalagnanam, Mohan and Singh, 2006; slide 27)**
- What it is: four functional stages that ICT changes:
  - Spend analysis: data aggregation, sourcing strategy.
  - Sourcing: catalogue buying and contract negotiation, RFx, supplier selection, vendor management.
  - Tactical procurement: requisitioning, approval workflow, supplier enablement, catalogue management.
  - Settlement: purchase ordering, invoicing, payables, receivables.
- Think of it as: the same caterer hunt, but run on an app with the bills paid automatically at the end.
- Spot it in a question: "requisitioning, approval workflow, supplier enablement, purchase ordering" → tactical procurement (Bank Week 3 Q6). "Invoicing" → settlement (Mock 1 Q7).

**What is reengineered (slide 28)**
- What it is: many new activities become possible with ICT. Unstructured decision-making activities are not specific activities. Structured decision-making activities are grouped by function and automated. Transaction processing without decisions is grouped together and automated.
- Think of it as: the routine forms get automated, and the judgement work is kept for people.
- Spot it in a question: "transaction processing with no decision making is automated" (Mock 1 Q8). Wrong options say such tasks stay manual or are removed.

**GE's Trading Process Network (TPN) (slides 22 and 23)**
- What it is: the buyer posts a request for proposal (RFP) on the internet for prequalified suppliers. Suppliers download it and submit bids electronically. The buyer evaluates the bids, negotiates online and places the order with the lowest bidder. The system also runs transaction processing (for example, automatically reconciling purchase orders with invoices in payment) and manages contract components.
- Results GE reported: transaction costs dropped by 30%; material costs reduced 5 to 20% by exploring unknown supplier bases and by spend analysis across the organisation; time saved of 6 to 8 days per month.
- Think of it as: a public tender board where only pre-approved bidders can post offers, and the cheapest one wins after negotiation.
- Spot it in a question: "lowest bidder" and "prequalified suppliers" → TPN. "Transaction cost dropped by 30 percent" → GE (Mock 1 Q6).

**Tata Steel e-procurement (recap, slide 24)**
- What it is: Metaljunction.com (steel e-market sponsored by SAIL, the Steel Authority of India Limited, and Tata Steel), an internal e-bidding solution, e-negotiation, and an online stock information system for VMI (vendor-managed inventory) suppliers. Full detail is in Week 1.
- Spot it in a question: "online stock information for vendor-managed inventory" → Tata's VMI system (Week 1 covers it in detail).

### E-procurement models

**E-procurement software**
- What it is: an internet-based application that lets employees buy goods from approved electronic catalogues under company buying rules, capturing purchasing data as they go. It can be coupled with the organisation's information system (slide 29).
- Think of it as: a company intranet shopping app that only shows approved items and checks your budget.
- Spot it in a question: "employees purchase from approved catalogues in accordance with buying rules" → e-procurement software (Mock 1 Q9 option A is the trap).

**Internet market exchanges**
- What it is: websites that bring multiple buyers and sellers together in one central virtual market space. External to the organisation's information system (slide 29; slide 57 for the servers).
- Think of it as: a virtual wholesale mandi, with stalls and a common price board.
- Spot it in a question: "one central virtual market space" → internet market exchange (Mock 1 Q9).

**Internet B2B (business-to-business) auctions**
- What it is: business-to-business auctions that give the buyer a much wider base of potential suppliers from around the world. Can be coupled with the organisation's information system.
- Think of it as: a global bidding room.
- Spot it in a question: "expanded base of potential suppliers from around the world".

**Internet purchasing consortia**
- What it is: a group of buyers pool their purchasing power to negotiate bigger discounts. External to the organisation's information system.
- Think of it as: several small shops buying rice together in one bulk order to get a better price.
- Spot it in a question: "gathers the purchasing power of many buyers" → consortia.

**Internet market exchange architecture (slide 57)**
- What it is: five parts. Buyers and sellers connect by internet to the exchange (exchange, auction, reverse auction). The B2B web portal (1): a market maker assigns trade platforms for specific products. The content manager server (2): aggregated product data is loaded into the live market server. The market generator server (3): collects and tracks bids from buyers and sellers from each platform. The live market server: runs the market. The post-trade market history server (4): after the market closes, emails buyers and sellers to confirm transactions, and notifies payment and fulfilment services.
- Think of it as: a market building with a front gate, a product catalogue room, a bid-tracking desk, the trading hall and a settlement office.
- Spot it in a question: "after the market closes, confirms transactions and notifies payment" → post-trade market history server.

### Spend analysis

**Spend analysis**
- What it is: the process of collecting, cleansing, classifying and analysing expenditure data to reduce procurement costs, improve efficiency and monitor compliance. It answers four questions: what was bought, when, from whom, and how much was paid (slide 33).
- Think of it as: a monthly review of every household expense, grouped by category and shop, to see where money leaks.
- Spot it in a question: "what was bought, when, with whom, how much" → spend analysis (Mock 1 Q10).

**Spend analysis use beyond purchasing (slide 34)**
- What it is: it also helps inventory management, budgeting and planning, and product development. Spend analysis sits inside spend management, which also covers commodity management and strategic sourcing.
- Think of it as: a household budget used to plan next year's grocery and gas budget too.
- Spot it in a question: "spend management" → the larger domain (Mock 2 Q8 → spend management).

**Aggregate spend view (slide 35)**
- What it is: a strategic activity that builds one view of total procurement spend across the organisation from transaction data. Aggregate spend by commodity, supplier and plant helps find cost-saving strategies. Example on the slide: find commodity classes or plants where fewer, preferred suppliers and higher volume could improve price negotiation. It helps decide the sourcing strategy.
- Think of it as: adding every family member's shopping into one list, so you can negotiate a bulk discount.
- Spot it in a question: "reduce suppliers and increase volume to preferred suppliers" → aggregate spend analysis for better negotiation.

**Functional requirements for spend analysis (slide 36)**
- What it is: three needs:
  - Data cleansing (scrubbing): aggregating data across the organisation; manual or semi-automatic; uses text similarity and machine learning; examples are supplier normalisation, commodity mapping and catalogue buying.
  - Data warehousing: an integrated view of all relevant data from different sources, needed to generate aggregated spend views and reports.
  - Analytics: data mining and optimisation.
- Example software on the slide: Ariba (spend analysis) and Ketera deem.
- Think of it as: wash the vegetables (cleansing), keep them in one store room (warehouse), then cook the menu (analytics).
- Spot it in a question: "integrates data from different sources into one view" → data warehousing (Mock 2 Q9). "Same vendor under different names" → supplier normalisation through data cleansing (Bank Week 3 Q7).

**Supplier normalisation**
- What it is: making several names for the same supplier into one standard supplier record. Part of data cleansing.
- Think of it as: "Ravi Traders", "R. Traders" and "Ravi T." all being one shopkeeper in your address book.
- Spot it in a question: "using varying supplier names for the same vendor" → supplier normalisation (Bank Week 3 Q7).

### Sourcing solutions

**Sourcing strategy**
- What it is: evaluate different sourcing options for each commodity class or other dimension, and identify potential cost savings (slide 37).
- Think of it as: deciding for each item whether to buy from one trusted shop, several shops, or through a bidding round.
- Spot it in a question: "evaluate sourcing options for each commodity class".

**Supplier scorecarding (slides 37 and 38)**
- What it is: analyses supplier performance against a company's strategic metrics. It identifies the top suppliers who will get future allocations (even at higher prices) and the suppliers that need tighter management. A scorecard may show: financial spending with each supplier, e-transactions to date, supplier performance (quality, service, logistics), and payment terms. It can include an evaluation dashboard, continuous monitoring, and feedback to the supplier with links. Minimum evaluations: quality, service and delivery (slide 39).
- Think of it as: a school report card for each supplier, with quality, service and delivery marks, shared with them.
- Spot it in a question: "good quality and service but slightly higher price" → continued allocation (Bank Week 3 Q8). Scorecards reward strategic performance.

**Catalogue buying (supplier driven, slide 40)**
- What it is: an internet-based application where employees buy goods from approved electronic catalogues under company buying rules, capturing purchasing data as they go. Example: Ariba (punch-out).
- Think of it as: a supermarket app with the company's approved shelf only.
- Spot it in a question: "approved electronic catalogues" and "supplier driven" → catalogue buying (Mock 1 Q12). Contrast with contract pricing, which is buyer driven.

**Contract pricing (buyer driven, through a third party, slide 41)**
- What it is: price negotiation based on a reverse auction or negotiation technique. Core ingredients: solicitation (RFx); a protocol for price discovery; contracts. Marketplaces run by third parties come in two kinds: vertical (industry specific, e.g., Metaljunction) and horizontal (no industry focus, e.g., Thomas Register).
- Think of it as: the buyer runs a bidding round, then signs the winning contract.
- Spot it in a question: "buyer driven", "reverse auction", "third party" → contract pricing. The "consignment inventory" option in Mock 3 Q5 is not a core ingredient.

**Vertical vs horizontal marketplaces**
- What it is: vertical = focused on one industry (Metaljunction for steel). Horizontal = no industry focus (Thomas Register).
- Think of it as: a specialist steel bazaar (vertical) versus a general directory of every business (horizontal).
- Spot it in a question: "single industry, such as steel" → vertical (Mock 1 Q11). "No specific industry focus" → horizontal (Mock 3 Q6, Thomas Register).

**E-procurement through third-party service providers (slide 58)**
- What it is: a provider in the middle offers buyers spend analysis, sourcing, contract management, supplier management and discovery of new sellers. It offers sellers discovery of new buyers, networking, sales contract management, order management, catalogue collaboration and supplier membership programmes. Cash management covers discount management, receivables financing, supply chain finance, invoice management and payment management. It also gives technical and training support.
- Think of it as: a full-service agency that runs the buying side and the selling side and handles the money.
- Spot it in a question: "a third party offers spend analysis, sourcing, contract management and invoice and payment management" → third-party service providers (Mock 3 Q14).

**Procurement through e-catalogues (slide 59)**
- What it is: an employee browses suppliers, finds products, orders items and confirms the order via the intranet. The order passes through the multi-supplier catalogue, corporate catalogue, order form, availability check and order entry, then catalogue content and workflow management, approval workflow and purchase order workflow. Then fulfilment (shipping, accounting, messaging) completes the order.
- Think of it as: an online grocery order that checks the store's stock and your boss's approval before the van leaves.
- Spot it in a question: "employees browse and order from approved catalogues via intranet" → e-catalogue procurement.

**Automating the tendering process (slide 56)**
- What it is: a sequence of messages between the department, the central purchase committee, the purchase section, suppliers, the store section and the finance and accounts (F&A) section. Steps: approval request and approval; auction setup request; enquiry generated; tender published; quotation request sent; bid response; bid opening; evaluation and selection (the highlighted block); purchase order; shipment; invoice; verification of goods; payment order; payment.
- Think of it as: an electronic tender file that moves from the requesting department, to the committee, to the suppliers, and back to the accounts office.
- Spot it in a question: "tender publication to evaluation and selection" → the automated tendering block.

### Solicitation and RFx

**Solicitation (slide 42)**
- What it is: seeking information, proposals or quotations from suppliers. Can be verbal, but quality and consistency improve when it is written or electronic.
- Think of it as: asking suppliers for their written offer, not just a phone call.
- Spot it in a question: "seeking information, proposals, quotations" → solicitation.

**RFI (Request for Information)**
- What it is: an open enquiry that spans the market and seeks broad data and understanding.
- Think of it as: a survey asking "what is out there?"
- Spot it in a question: "open enquiry that spans the market", "broad data" → RFI (Mock 2 Q10).

**RFQ (Request for Quotation)**
- What it is: an opportunity for potential suppliers to competitively cost the final chosen solution or solutions.
- Think of it as: "give me your price for these exact items".
- Spot it in a question: "competitively cost", "quotation" → RFQ (Bank Week 3 Q5 uses RFQ to multiple suppliers).

**RFP (Request for Proposal)**
- What it is: based on the RFI, a requirements-based request for specific solutions to the sourcing problem.
- Think of it as: "here is my problem; tell me how you would solve it".
- Spot it in a question: "based on the RFI", "specific solutions" → RFP.

**RFx (collective term, slide 43)**
- What it is: RFI, RFQ and RFP together. Each is a document and a way for a buyer to specify the requirements of a purchase along several dimensions from many suppliers. RFx applications support creating and editing documents in the same way as paper, specify business rules, and offer decision-making support to create and evaluate complex RFQs and bids.
- Think of it as: the family's standard invitation template for three kinds of enquiry.
- Spot it in a question: "collectively referred to as RFx" → any of RFI, RFQ or RFP.

**RFx process (slide 44)**
- What it is: (1) the buyer submits requirements, bid type and business rules; (2) the RFx document is created and edited; (3) suppliers submit RFx bids; (4) RFQs are evaluated and the allocation is made to the buyer or supplier.
- Think of it as: a tender with a template, a deadline, a judging panel and a winner.
- Spot it in a question: "business rules" in the RFx document; "evaluation of RFQs" comes before allocation.

**Characteristics of RFx systems (slide 45)**
- What it is: the sourcing platform should make the RFx process as easy as possible for everyone; be versatile enough for goods and services, and for both direct and indirect spend; and support a wide range of RFx types and sizes, from simple RFIs to complex RFPs.
- Think of it as: a single app that handles small and large orders, for both shopping and service bookings.
- Spot it in a question: "both goods and services, both direct and indirect spend" → versatility (Mock 2 Q11).

### Reverse auctions and price discovery

**Reverse auction**
- What it is: the buyer posts the need and suppliers bid the price down. The lowest acceptable bid wins.
- Think of it as: eBay upside down. The buyer is the auctioneer and sellers compete to go lower.
- Spot it in a question: "suppliers bid down the price", "buyer wants the lowest price" → reverse auction. Contrast with an auction broker in Week 1 (sellers bid up).

**Basic reverse auction settings (slide 46)**
- What it is: multiple quantities of a line item; multiple line items; start and reserve prices; partial-quantity bids and award allocations (a supplier can bid on part of the quantity); bundled bids (several items in one offer).
- Think of it as: a buyer asking for 500 bags of cement at 3 sites, with a maximum price and the right to split the order.
- Spot it in a question: "bid on only part of the quantity" → partial quantity bids and award allocations (Mock 2 Q12).

**Advanced reverse auction formats (slide 47)**
- What it is:
  - Combinatorial auction: suppliers mix bundled bids with un-bundled bids (Mock 2 Q13).
  - Volume discount auction: suppliers set price discounts at certain quantities (Mock 1 Q13).
  - Multi-attribute reverse auction: the winner is decided by a score, using the buyer's weights for price, quantity and other attributes (not just price) (Bank Week 3 Q9).
- Think of it as: a combination tender (combinatorial), a bulk-discount tender (volume discount), and a scoring tender where price is one of several marks (multi-attribute).
- Spot it in a question: "weighted combination of price, delivery, warranty, service" → multi-attribute (Bank Week 3 Q9). "Mix bundled and un-bundled bids" → combinatorial. "Discount at a certain quantity" → volume discount.

**Single-round price discovery (slide 48)**
- What it is: RFQs are often used in a single round, similar to a one-shot sealed-bid auction. Winners are selected, based on the bid evaluation engine's recommendations, after all bids are in. Also called online tendering.
- Think of it as: a sealed envelope tender: everybody submits once and the committee opens them.
- Spot it in a question: "after all the bids are in", "one shot sealed bid", "online tendering" → single round (Bank Week 3 Q10 contrast; Mock 1 Q14).

**Multiple-round price discovery (slide 48, 49)**
- What it is: in a negotiation context, the process runs in rounds. After each round, suppliers may reformulate their bids based on information about the winning bids (feedback from the auctioneer). Also called reverse auctions.
- Think of it as: a vegetable-market haggle: you lower your bid after you hear what others are offering.
- Spot it in a question: "suppliers revise bids repeatedly after feedback" → multiple-round price discovery (Bank Week 3 Q10).
- Process flow (slide 49): "new bids?" → if YES: allocation (winner determination and quantity matching) → pricing (settlement price computation) → signalling for bid reformulation (price signalling feeds back to the new-bids check). If NO, the process ends. Iterative negotiations need price signalling.

### Contract management

**Contract (slide 50)**
- What it is: a legal document with the price of the item(s) to be procured and the other terms and conditions. Once signed, it is used to procure the contracted items using the negotiated prices and terms.
- Think of it as: the signed rate card with the caterer, listing the price per plate and the conditions.
- Spot it in a question: "legal documents containing price and terms".

**Contract management requirements (slide 50)**
- What it is: a sourcing platform should generate a contract from the preceding RFx and auction negotiations, support contract negotiations, and monitor compliance with the business commitments over time.
- Think of it as: the caterer's contract generated from the bid, then checked every month to see the caterer kept the promises.
- Spot it in a question: "monitor compliance over time" → contract management.

**What current contract management software does (slide 51)**
- What it is: sends an alert when a contract is soon to expire; monitors the buyer's purchase volume commitments and warns if the buyer is in danger of buying under the minimum quantity in the period; sends notifications when a supplier violates a delivery commitment.
- Think of it as: a reminder app that warns you when a membership is about to expire.
- Spot it in a question: "alert when a contract is about to expire" → alert notification (Mock 3 Q7). "Warn if buying below the minimum" → purchase volume commitment alert.

**What current contract management software does not do (slide 51)**
- What it is: the contract commitments to be monitored must be manually picked out of the negotiated legal text into a structured form. The business process data and raw transaction data needed to check whether commitments are kept or broken are also captured manually.
- Think of it as: a clerk must type the contract's clauses into a checklist before the app can do anything.
- Spot it in a question: "commitments must be manually extracted from the legal wording" → the limitation (Mock 2 Q14). Trap: the alert and notification features are things the software does do.

### Spot the e-procurement risks and barriers

**Internal business risks (slide 61)**
- What it is: availability of appropriate resources for successful implementation; integration with the existing information infrastructure; significant investments.
- Think of it as: buying a new kitchen that must fit the existing gas line and needs a lot of money.
- Spot it in a question: "significant investment and integration with the existing infrastructure" → internal business risk (Mock 3 Q11).

**External business risks (slide 62)**
- What it is: cooperation of suppliers (they must also invest in technology, and there is no guarantee of return on investment); e-catalogues in the required formats, reflecting custom pricing and contract agreements, with regular updates; the buyer-supplier relationship.
- Think of it as: a caterer who must buy a new oven to work with your app and cannot be sure it will pay off.
- Spot it in a question: "supplier cooperation" or "catalogues updated regularly" → external risk.

**Technology risks (slide 63)**
- What it is: a lack of clear understanding of which e-procurement technology suits each company; no widely accepted solution for integrating different e-procurement software across the supply chain; no clear, open standards for inter-organisational e-procurement.
- Think of it as: two phones that cannot share photos because they use different chargers and file formats.
- Spot it in a question: "no widely accepted standard", "integration across the supply chain" → technology risk (Mock 3 Q12).

**Process risks (slide 64)**
- What it is: organisations must be confident that unauthorised actions will not disrupt production or other supply chain activities. The adoption challenge is to show non-users that the technology does not undermine control, security or privacy; is not so complex that low-skill organisations cannot use it; and that the new business model gives the right incentives to all supply chain partners.
- Think of it as: a bank that must prove its online account is safe before customers switch.
- Spot it in a question: "unauthorised actions could disrupt production" → process risk (Mock 1 Q15).

**Barriers: e-procurement software and internet exchanges (slide 65)**
- What it is: software problems integrating with existing systems; lack of a common standard for e-commerce software development; lack of supplier access to the e-procurement system and lack of supplier investment in catalogue development. Internet exchanges: not enough suppliers for a liquid marketplace; supplier reluctance where price is the main focus; supplier reluctance because they lose control over how their brand and product features are shown.
- Think of it as: an empty market with no sellers, and sellers who fear being judged on price alone.
- Spot it in a question: "not enough suppliers to create a liquid marketplace" → exchange barrier.

**Barriers: e-auctions and purchasing consortia (slide 66)**
- What it is: e-auctions: organisational discomfort with auctions, as opposed to honouring commitments to supplier partnering and consolidation; downward price pressure that reduces customer service or quality; inability to identify potential items for auction. Consortia: pricing not significantly better than without a consortium; getting enough vendors into the process; ensuring conformance to state laws and regulations.
- Think of it as: a family that is uncomfortable haggling with the regular grocer, and a group purchase where the discount turns out to be small.
- Spot it in a question: "price pressure reduces service" → e-auction barrier. "Pricing not significantly better" → consortia barrier (Mock 3 Q13).

### Customer relationship management (CRM)

**CRM (customer relationship management)**
- What it is: focuses on acquiring and retaining profitable customers through marketing, sales and service processes (slide 70).
- Think of it as: a shopkeeper's memory for regular customers: who they are, what they bought, and what to offer next.
- Spot it in a question: "acquiring and retaining profitable customers" → CRM (Mock 2 Q3).

**CRM purpose (slide 71)**
- What it is: two objectives. Give the organisation and all its customer-facing staff a single, complete view of every customer at every touchpoint and across all channels. Give the customer a single, complete view of the company and its extended channels.
- Think of it as: every shop assistant can see your full order history on one screen, no matter which counter you walk to.
- Spot it in a question: "single, complete view of every customer at every touchpoint" → CRM purpose.

**Touchpoints (slide 73)**
- What it is: every contact point with a customer: telephone, fax, e-mail, the company website, retail stores, kiosks, and personal contact. All write to one common customer database.
- Think of it as: every counter in the mall feeding the same register.
- Spot it in a question: "information from all contact points into a common database" → CRM contact and account management.

**Application cluster: contact and account management (slide 73)**
- What it is: captures and tracks relevant data about every past and planned contact with prospects and customers, and other life-cycle events. Stores it in a common customer database available across the company.
- Think of it as: a diary that records every call, visit and birthday.
- Spot it in a question: "every past and planned contact" → contact and account management (Mock 3 Q4).

**Application cluster: sales (slide 74)**
- What it is:
  - Cross-selling: a customer of one product may want a related product (e.g., a camera buyer offered a memory card).
  - Up-selling: selling a new or existing customer a better product.
  - Real-time access to a single common view of the customer: checks account status and history before a sales call; alerts a salesperson to unresolved service, delivery or payment problems.
- Think of it as: a bookshop assistant who suggests the second book of the same author (cross-sell) or the hardcover edition (up-sell).
- Spot it in a question: "related product" → cross-selling (Mock 3 Q9). "Better product" → up-selling.

**Application cluster: marketing and fulfilment (slide 75)**
- What it is: direct marketing campaigns that automatically identify leads for targeted marketing; scheduling and tracking direct marketing mailings; capturing and managing prospect and customer response data in the CRM database; analysing the customer and business value of campaigns; fulfilling responses by quickly scheduling sales contacts and sending product information.
- Think of it as: a targeted leaflet campaign, with the response cards logged and followed up.
- Spot it in a question: "direct marketing campaigns", "lead identification" → marketing and fulfilment.

**Application cluster: customer service and support (slide 76)**
- What it is: real-time access to the customer database for service staff; creating, assigning and managing service requests. Call-centre software routes calls based on the skills of service staff. Help-desk software provides service data and suggestions for solving problems. Web-based self-service gives personalised support information, with the option of further help by online or phone.
- Think of it as: the bank helpline that sends your call to the agent who knows loans, and a FAQ page you can use first.
- Spot it in a question: "routing calls based on the skills of service staff" → call-centre software. "Personalised support information on the web, with the option to phone" → web-based self-service (Mock 3 Q10).

**Application cluster: retention and loyalty programmes (slide 77)**
- What it is: helps identify, reward and market to the most loyal and profitable customers. Uses data mining and other analytical tools.
- Think of it as: a frequent-flyer programme that finds your best customers and rewards them.
- Spot it in a question: "identify, reward and market to the most loyal and profitable customers" → retention and loyalty programmes.

**Three phases of CRM (slide 78)**
- What it is: acquire (getting new customers); enhance (supporting existing customers); retain (making the customer loyal). ICT support shows functional solutions: direct marketing, cross-sell and up-sell, proactive service, sales force automation, customer support.
- Think of it as: winning a new guest, looking after the guest during the stay, and making sure they come back next year.
- Spot it in a question: "supporting existing customers" → enhance (Mock 3 Q15). "Getting new customers" → acquire. "Making the customer loyal" → retain.

**Causes of CRM failure (slide 79)**
- What it is: lack of senior management sponsorship; improper change management; elongated projects that take on too much, too fast; lack of or poor integration with core business systems; lack of end-user incentives, leading to poor adoption.
- Think of it as: a new school timetable that the principal never supported, introduced too fast, not linked to the existing register, and that teachers have no reason to use.
- Spot it in a question: "too much, too fast" or "no senior sponsorship" → CRM failure cause.

**Trend: operational CRM (slide 80)**
- What it is: supports customer interaction with convenience through many channels (phone, fax, e-mail, chat, mobile devices); synchronises interactions across all channels; makes the company easier to do business with.
- Think of it as: the same service whether you call, chat or walk in.
- Spot it in a question: "many channels, consistent across channels" → operational CRM.

**Trend: analytical CRM (slide 81)**
- What it is: extracts in-depth customer history, preferences and profitability from a data warehouse and other databases. Lets the firm analyse, predict and derive customer value and behaviour, and forecast demand. Approaches customers with offers tailored to their needs.
- Think of it as: a shopkeeper's notebook scaled up into a data warehouse, used to predict who will buy what.
- Spot it in a question: "extracts history from a data warehouse to predict demand and tailor offers" → analytical CRM (Mock 2 Q15).

**Trend: collaborative CRM (slide 82)**
- What it is: enables easy collaboration with customers, suppliers and partners; improves efficiency and integration through the supply chain; greater responsiveness to customer needs through sourcing products and services outside the enterprise.
- Think of it as: the caterer, the florist and the customer all working on one shared wedding plan.
- Spot it in a question: "collaboration with customers, suppliers and partners" → collaborative CRM.

**Trend: portal-based CRM (slide 83)**
- What it is: gives all users the tools and information that fit their roles and preferences; empowers employees to respond quickly; provides instant access to link and use all internal and external customer information.
- Think of it as: every staff member gets a personal screen with exactly what they need to help the customer.
- Spot it in a question: "tools fitted to each user's role" → portal-based CRM (enterprise portal link).

**Integrated CRM solution (slide 78 diagram)**
- What it is: partner, company and customer connected through shared customer data and collaborative service on the internet.
- Think of it as: the three families of a wedding sharing one album and one guest list.

## Don't mix these up

| These look alike | How to tell them apart | Tie-breaker word |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate website vs enterprise portal | Website: public, informational, interface to the public. Portal: staff and selected partners, personalised, integrates MIS, DSS and other systems. | "public" vs "employees" |
| Corporate website feature: splash page vs site map vs store locator | Splash page = entry point to the home page. Site map = list of the site's sections. Store locator = nearby shops. | "entry point" → splash page |
| Internal vs external enterprise portal applications | Internal: e-mail, project sites, discussion groups, HR self-service, databases, DSS, KM systems. External: industry news, industry discussion groups, customer and supplier extranet links. | "project sites" → internal; "news" → external |
| Hits vs page views vs visits vs click paths vs traffic sources | Hits = file requests. Page views = pages seen. Visits = sessions. Click paths = route through pages. Traffic sources = where visitors came from. | "came from" → traffic sources |
| Web analytics vs web design | Analytics measures usage to improve design. Design is the look and layout itself. | "measure and report usage" → analytics |
| Procurement vs purchasing vs sourcing | Procurement = whole process, including receipt and checking quality and timing. Purchasing = buying the right item, quantity, price, source and time. Sourcing = finding and engaging suppliers. | "receive and check" → procurement; "find" → sourcing; "buy" → purchasing |
| Sourcing vs settlement | Sourcing finds and negotiates suppliers. Settlement pays: purchase ordering, invoicing, payables, receivables. | "invoice" → settlement |
| Spend analysis vs spend management | Spend analysis is the data activity. Spend management is the wider domain: spend analysis + commodity management + strategic sourcing. | "wider domain" → spend management |
| Data cleansing vs data warehousing vs analytics | Cleansing fixes names and codes (supplier normalisation). Warehousing gathers all data into one view. Analytics finds patterns and optimal choices. | "different names" → cleansing; "one view" → warehousing |
| Supplier normalisation vs supplier scorecarding | Normalisation: one standard name for one supplier. Scorecarding: rates performance on quality, service, delivery. | "name" vs "rating" |
| Traditional purchasing vs reengineered purchasing | Traditional: four manual stages (define, select, contract, evaluate). Reengineered: spend analysis, sourcing, tactical procurement, settlement, automated with ICT. | "automated" → reengineered |
| Tactical procurement vs settlement | Tactical: requisitioning, approval workflow, supplier enablement, catalogue management. Settlement: purchase ordering, invoicing, payables, receivables. | "approval" → tactical; "invoice" → settlement |
| Catalogue buying vs contract pricing | Catalogue buying: supplier-driven, employees buy from approved catalogues. Contract pricing: buyer-driven, bidding and contract through a third party. | "approved catalogue" vs "reverse auction" |
| Supplier-driven vs buyer-driven sourcing | Supplier-driven: the supplier fills the catalogue (catalogue buying). Buyer-driven: the buyer sets the requirement and runs a bid (contract pricing). | "who drives" |
| E-procurement software vs internet market exchange | Software: used inside the company for approved buying, coupled with its IS. Exchange: a central virtual market external to the company's IS. | "approved catalogues" vs "central virtual market space" |
| Internet market exchange vs internet B2B auction vs internet purchasing consortia | Exchange: many buyers and sellers meet in one virtual space. B2B auction: bidding with a wider supplier base from around the world. Consortium: buyers pool power to negotiate discounts. | "one space" / "bid" / "pool power" |
| Vertical vs horizontal marketplace | Vertical: one industry (Metaljunction for steel). Horizontal: no industry focus (Thomas Register). | "single industry" → vertical |
| Third-party service provider vs internet purchasing consortia | Third-party provider: a service firm offering spend, sourcing, contract and payment services for buyers and sellers. Consortium: buyers pool purchasing power themselves. | "third party" vs "buyers pool" |
| RFI vs RFQ vs RFP | RFI: open enquiry, broad market data. RFQ: competitive pricing of the chosen solution. RFP: specific solutions to the sourcing problem, based on the RFI. | "open enquiry" / "price" / "solutions" |
| Solicitation vs RFx | Solicitation is the general act of asking suppliers. RFx is the family of three documents (RFI, RFQ, RFP). | "documents" → RFx |
| Reverse auction vs auction broker (Week 1) | Reverse auction: buyer posts, suppliers bid down. Auction broker: sellers sell, bids go up, broker takes a commission. | "who is buying?" |
| Single-round vs multiple-round price discovery | Single round: one sealed submission, winners selected after all bids are in. Multiple round: suppliers revise bids after feedback on winning bids (reverse auction). | "after all bids are in" vs "repeatedly after feedback" |
| Combinatorial vs volume discount vs multi-attribute auction | Combinatorial: mixes bundled and un-bundled bids. Volume discount: discounts at certain quantities. Multi-attribute: winner by a score of price and other attributes. | "mix bundled" / "quantity discount" / "score" |
| Partial quantity bids vs bundled bids | Partial quantity: a supplier bids on part of the quantity. Bundled: several items in one offer. | "part of the quantity" vs "several items together" |
| Start price vs reserve price | Start price is where the bidding opens. Reserve price is the buyer's limit (the highest price the buyer will accept). | "opens" → start; "limit" → reserve |
| Contract vs purchase order | A contract is the legal agreement with price and terms. A purchase order is one order placed under the contract. | "legal document" vs "order placed" |
| Contract alerts vs contract monitoring limits | Software does send alerts (expiry, minimum volume, delivery violation). It does not extract the commitments from the legal text automatically. | "manually" → limitation |
| Risk types: internal vs external vs technology vs process | Internal: resources, integration, investment (our own firm). External: suppliers and catalogues. Technology: which tech, standards, integration. Process: unauthorised actions, trust, skills, incentives. | "our investment" / "suppliers" / "standards" / "control and trust" |
| Process risk vs internal business risk | Process: unauthorised actions disrupting production; needs evidence of control and simplicity. Internal business: resources, infrastructure fit and money. | "unauthorised" → process |
| Barriers: exchanges vs e-auctions vs consortia | Exchanges: liquidity and brand control. E-auctions: discomfort with auctions, price pressure. Consortia: pricing not better, few vendors, legal conformance. | "liquid marketplace" / "price pressure" / "not better" |
| Corporate website vs CRM | Website is a public information face. CRM is the internal system for managing each customer's relationship. | "information to public" vs "manage customer" |
| CRM acquire vs enhance vs retain | Acquire: new customers. Enhance: support existing customers with more products or service. Retain: keep them loyal. | "new" / "existing" / "loyal" |
| Cross-selling vs up-selling | Cross-selling: a related product. Up-selling: a better product. | "related" vs "better" |
| Direct marketing vs customer service | Direct marketing: campaigns to find and reach leads. Customer service: helping existing customers with requests. | "campaign" vs "request" |
| Operational vs analytical vs collaborative vs portal-based CRM | Operational: consistent channel interactions. Analytical: data-warehouse analysis and prediction. Collaborative: working with customers, suppliers and partners. Portal-based: role-based tools for each employee. | "channels" / "predict" / "partners" / "role" |
| Call-centre software vs help-desk software vs web self-service | Call centre: routes calls by agent skill. Help desk: suggests fixes from service data. Web self-service: personalised online help with an option to phone. | "routing calls" / "suggestions" / "online" |
| Loyalty programme vs direct marketing | Loyalty programme rewards and targets the best customers. Direct marketing reaches prospects with campaigns. | "reward" vs "campaign" |
| Analytical CRM vs data mining | Analytical CRM is the trend (using a warehouse to predict and tailor). Data mining is a technique (clustering, classification, association) used in it. | "trend" vs "technique" |

## How this week gets asked

**Question patterns you will see**
- "Which platform or tool fits this scenario": enterprise portal vs corporate website; splash page; web metric.
- "Which term does this definition describe": procurement, purchasing, sourcing, spend analysis, solicitation, RFI/RFQ/RFP.
- "Which auction or price-discovery format fits": partial quantity, combinatorial, volume discount, multi-attribute, single round, multiple round.
- "Which CRM phase, application cluster or trend": acquire/enhance/retain; cross-sell; call-centre routing; analytical trend.
- "Which risk or barrier": process, technology, internal, external; liquidity, price pressure, consortium pricing.
- "Which reengineered stage or traditional stage": tactical procurement vs settlement, supplier evaluation.
- "Which is NOT": a listed item (Mock 3 Q5 consignment inventory; Bank Week 3 Q4 procurement vs purchasing; Mock 1 Q3 expediting).

**Distractor traps for this week**
- Near-synonyms in the procurement stages: purchasing vs procurement vs sourcing. "Verify delivery and quality" is procurement, not purchasing. "Find suppliers" is sourcing.
- RFI vs RFQ vs RFP: RFI is an open enquiry (broad). RFQ is a price request for a chosen solution. RFP is a requirements-based proposal request, written after the RFI.
- Reverse auction vs auction broker (Week 1): in a reverse auction the buyer buys and suppliers bid down. Do not pick the Week 1 eBay model.
- Single round vs multiple round: "after all bids are in" means single round. "after feedback, revise bids" means multiple round.
- Vertical vs horizontal marketplaces: "single industry" is vertical; "no industry focus" is horizontal (Thomas Register).
- Contract software: a wrong option may say the software does the extraction of commitments by itself. The lecture says it does not.
- CRM phases: enhance means supporting existing customers. Retain means loyalty. Acquire means new customers. Do not swap them.
- Risk category words: "unauthorised actions" = process; "significant investment" = internal business; "no open standards" = technology; "supplier cooperation" = external business.
- Corporate website vs enterprise portal: internal staff or selected partners = portal; the public = website.
- Absolute words: "eliminates all", "guaranteed return", "fully automated". The lecture says there is no guarantee on return on investment for suppliers (slide 62). Look for these absolutes.

**Rules to use in the exam**
1. If the question says "single personalised interface for employees" → enterprise portal.
2. If the question says "public, informational, investors, press releases" → corporate website.
3. If it says "from search engines, social media, referral sites" → traffic sources.
4. If it says "finding, evaluating and engaging suppliers" → sourcing. "Right quality, quantity, price, source and time" → purchasing. "Receipt and quality check" → procurement.
5. If it says "bids down the price, buyer runs the auction" → reverse auction. "Bid after feedback, several rounds" → multiple-round price discovery. "All bids in, then winner chosen" → single round.
6. If it says "weights for price, delivery and service" → multi-attribute reverse auction.
7. If it says "employees buy from approved catalogues" → catalogue buying (supplier driven).
8. If it says "a third party runs spend, sourcing, contracts and payments" → third-party service providers.
9. If it says "cleanse supplier names, one view of spend" → data cleansing / supplier normalisation, then warehousing.
10. If it says "customer is offered a related product" → cross-selling; "better product" → up-selling.

## Practice walk-through

**Q1.** An organisation wants employees to reach HR self-service, decision support systems, project websites and corporate databases through one personalised interface. Which platform?
- Answer: B) Enterprise portal
- Why: An enterprise portal integrates MIS, DSS and other tools into one personalised interface for internal users (Corporate website vs enterprise portal cards; slides 4 and 5).
- Trap: A) Corporate website. A corporate website faces the public and is not used for internal HR self-service.

**Q2.** Employees spend less time searching across unrelated websites after a portal is launched. Which benefit is most directly reflected?
- Answer: D) Easier access to relevant resources through a centralized information gateway
- Why: The portal is one gateway to internal and external resources, and benefit list on slide 8 includes easy access to key resources.
- Trap: A) Improved collaboration with external suppliers through automated bidding. That is an e-procurement benefit, not a search-time saving.

**Q3.** A web analyst wants to know whether visitors arrive from search engines, social media or referral websites. Which metric?
- Answer: B) Traffic sources
- Why: Traffic sources show where visitors came from (Web metrics card; slide 13).
- Trap: D) Click paths. Click paths show the route through the site after arrival, not where visitors came from.

**Q4.** Which activity is part of procurement but not fully covered by purchasing and sourcing?
- Answer: D) Verifying that ordered materials are delivered on schedule and meet agreed specifications
- Why: Burt's definition of procurement includes ensuring that what is required is received on time, in quantity and quality (Procurement definition card; slide 18).
- Trap: B) Obtaining quotations and negotiating prices. That sits inside sourcing and purchasing, so it is covered by the narrower concepts.

**Q5.** A team issues an RFQ to several suppliers, compares bids against business rules, and discusses revisions with shortlisted suppliers. Which functional area is primarily involved?
- Answer: B) Sourcing
- Why: Contract negotiation, RFx and supplier selection sit in the sourcing column of the reengineered process (Reengineered purchasing card; slide 27).
- Trap: C) Settlement. Settlement covers invoicing and payment after the purchase, not bid comparison.

**Q6.** A company has automated requisitioning, approval workflows, supplier enablement and purchase ordering. These belong to:
- Answer: C) Tactical Procurement
- Why: Requisitioning, approval workflow, supplier enablement and catalogue management are tactical procurement (slide 27).
- Trap: B) Settlement. Settlement starts after purchase ordering, with invoicing and payables.

**Q7.** A company holds data from different plants that use different supplier names for the same vendor. Before meaningful spend reports, what is most critical?
- Answer: C) Supplier normalization through data cleansing
- Why: Making one standard supplier name from many variants is supplier normalisation within data cleansing (Spend analysis functional requirements; slide 36).
- Trap: A) Reverse auction support through data cleansing. Reverse auctions do not fix supplier names.

**Q8.** A supplier charges slightly higher prices than competitors but is excellent on quality, service and delivery. What would supplier scorecarding most likely recommend?
- Answer: B) Continued allocation due to strategic performance metrics
- Why: Scorecards identify top suppliers who will keep future allocations even at higher prices (Supplier scorecarding card; slides 37 and 38).
- Trap: A) Immediate supplier replacement. Scorecarding would recommend tighter management for weak performers, not replacing a strong one.

**Q9.** A buyer evaluates suppliers using weighted price, delivery time, warranty and service quality. Which auction format?
- Answer: D) Multi-attribute reverse auction
- Why: The winner is decided by a score that uses the buyer's weights for several attributes, not just price (Advanced reverse auction formats; slide 47).
- Trap: C) Online tendering. Online tendering is a single-round process and does not score several attributes by weight.

**Q10.** A sourcing platform lets suppliers revise bids repeatedly after feedback on the current leading bids. This is:
- Answer: C) Multiple-round price discovery
- Why: Bids are reformulated after each round based on feedback (Price discovery card; slides 48 and 49).
- Trap: A) Single-round tendering. In single round, suppliers bid once and the winner is picked after all bids are in.

## 60-second recap

- Corporate website = public, informational. Enterprise portal = one personalised gateway for staff and selected partners, combining MIS, DSS and EIS.
- Web analytics measures navigation, content and users. Metrics: hits, page views, visits, click paths, traffic sources. Google Analytics uses page tagging.
- Sourcing finds and engages suppliers. Purchasing buys the right quality, quantity, price, source and time. Procurement includes receipt and quality checks.
- Traditional purchasing: define, select, contract, evaluate (manual). Reengineered: spend analysis, sourcing, tactical procurement, settlement (ICT-automated).
- Four e-procurement models: software (inside IS, approved catalogues), market exchanges (central virtual market), B2B auctions (wider supplier base), purchasing consortia (pooled buyers).
- Spend analysis asks what, when, with whom and how much. It needs data cleansing (supplier normalisation), data warehousing and analytics.
- Catalogue buying is supplier driven. Contract pricing is buyer driven, through reverse auctions and RFx.
- RFI = open enquiry. RFQ = price for a chosen solution. RFP = requirements-based proposal after an RFI.
- Reverse auctions: basic settings (partial quantity, bundled, start and reserve prices); advanced (combinatorial, volume discount, multi-attribute).
- Single round = one submission, winners picked after all bids. Multiple round = repeated bids after feedback.
- Contract software sends alerts; it still needs commitments typed in manually from the legal text.
- E-procurement risks: internal business, external business, technology, process.
- CRM = acquire and retain profitable customers. Three phases: acquire, enhance, retain.
- CRM clusters: contact and account, sales (cross-sell, up-sell), marketing and fulfilment, customer service and support, retention and loyalty.
- CRM trends: operational, analytical, collaborative, portal-based. CRM failure causes: no sponsorship, poor change management, too much too fast, poor integration, no user incentives.
